MILab applied projects

Six strands of applied research in conversational avatars, from serving delay and audio-driven 3D faces to embedded execution.

Research Assistant · North South University · March 2025–present

Our work at MILab connects research questions with the practical demands of conversational visual systems, from a slow server to an embedded real-time device. I took primary technical responsibility for the early pipeline, representation and bridge work. The real-time Orin customization and the retrieval and tool work were major team efforts.

Conversational pipelines and response delay

The first thing a listener notices in a conversational avatar is not image quality but how long they wait. This work built the serving path — speech synthesis, generation and playback queues — and then went after the delay that path produced.

I held primary technical responsibility for this early pipeline and latency work; the robot deployment below was a team effort.

MuseTalk 512×512 training and high-resolution development

Training and high-resolution avatar development built on the upstream MuseTalk foundation, raising output resolution while keeping the generation step inside the pipeline’s timing budget.

MuseTalk/LatentSync conversational pipeline and multi-GPU serving

Chunked execution, persistent text-to-speech, separate speech and video queues, and local and WebRTC playback paths, followed by a multi-GPU server.

In that earlier development I estimated response delay at roughly 30 seconds at first and about 4 seconds on the later multi-GPU server. Both are historical development estimates for that server, measured end to end from a finished user turn; they are not benchmarks and do not carry over to the later Orin system.

Primary technical responsibility for substantial parts of the integration and latency work.

Streaming avatar on a humanoid robot face

The streaming avatar was taken off the screen and onto a physical robot head built by our industry partner, RB Labs, whose Robert humanoid robot provides the hardware and the surrounding product system. Our side was the face: driving the rendered head from streaming speech and fitting it to the robot's own display and timing constraints. The robot, its hardware and the partner platform are theirs; this was a team effort at the lab, and I worked on the avatar generation and streaming side of it.

Team effort with an industry partner; my part was the streaming avatar face.

Audio-driven 3D avatars and blendshape control

Before the Gaussian and renderer work, our 3D avatars were driven through NVIDIA's Audio2Face. That route gives per-frame vertex motion for a face mesh, but a character rig is controlled by a small set of named blendshape channels, so the animation has to be recovered in those terms before an avatar can use it. Audio2Face is NVIDIA's software. I held primary technical responsibility for this work.

Audio2Face-driven 3D avatars in the browser

A pre-trained Audio2Face model was integrated with a Three.js viewer so that generated speech could drive a 3D head in a browser session. The practical difficulty was in the plumbing rather than the model: model formats and texturing, converting and mapping the vertex animation onto the delivered mesh, and keeping playback aligned with the audio that produced it. A visible delay between audio and animation remained in this early integration.

Recovering blendshape controls from vertex animation

The network emits per-frame vertex offsets for the whole face mesh; what a rig needs is a weight for each of 52 standard blendshape controls. I framed the recovery as a regularised inverse problem — find the weights whose blendshape combination best reconstructs the frame's vertex offsets — and solved it frame by frame with an iterative shrinkage-thresholding scheme, deriving the step size from the system matrix so the solve stayed numerically stable instead of diverging.

The pipeline ran end to end and wrote control tracks in the target format, but the recovered weights came out heavily damped against the reference output. The explanation recorded at the time is that the configuration's regularisation strengths are tuned for the vendor's own solver and do not transfer directly to this one. We did not get to test it.

Face representation and motion stability

A speech-driven avatar can be perfectly in time and still look wrong. These studies asked which representation and fitting choices keep facial motion stable, and treated the failures as evidence rather than noise.

I held primary technical responsibility for these investigations, including the ones that did not work out.

Head-wobble stabilization investigations

Controlled training and rendering variants isolating unwanted head motion in speech-driven Gaussian avatars. Failure cases were kept in the record; the diagnostics describe training behaviour and do not stand in for a perceptual measure of stability.

Pixel3DMM fitting and stabilization

Upstream Pixel3DMM used as a fitting foundation to examine how face representation and motion estimation affect stability. Establishing the fitting path was a starting point for that comparison rather than a demonstrated general fix.

Primary technical responsibility for the fitting and representation pathway.

Custom FLAME articulation

Custom FLAME integration and controlled articulation studies, separating fitting, motion and rendering so that each could be varied on its own. One articulation-supervision proposal from this line was stopped on review rather than carried forward; the wider FLAME and avatar work continued.

Primary technical responsibility for this early articulation work.

Speech-to-motion bridges and renderer cost

A streaming speech model and a face renderer work in different natural units. Each pairing has to agree on chunk boundaries, buffering and what a late chunk means, so the bridges are separate pieces of work rather than one adapter reused four times. A parallel strand measured what the renderer itself costs.

I held primary technical responsibility for these bridges and for the distillation study.

Gaussian avatar–Moshi integration

Moshi/Mimi speech representations connected to a GaussianTalker-based avatar, with streaming execution across the two components.

Primary technical responsibility for the integration and streaming development.

Moshi–MuseTalk integration

Streaming adapters and execution infrastructure between conversational speech-model output and MuseTalk face generation, with attention to chunk boundaries and buffering.

IMTalker renderer distillation

Fixed-identity teacher–student renderer development with component-level benchmarks designed to separate renderer cost from end-to-end conversational response time. Stage-level results stay tied to the conditions they were measured under and do not imply a change a user would perceive.

Primary technical responsibility for the renderer work and benchmark design.

Embedded real-time execution on Jetson AGX Orin

Moving the pipeline onto a single embedded device changes which costs matter. Export and precision decisions that are free on a server become the constraint, and the system has to hold a conversation within one power budget.

Jetson AGX Orin export and TensorRT optimization

Export tooling, engine integration, precision and graph checks, and component-level profiling for constrained embedded execution. Timings from this work belong to the specific hardware and power mode they were taken in and are not transferable to another configuration.

Primary technical responsibility for the export and optimization paths.

PersonaPlex–IMTalker real-time customization on Orin

Customization of the PersonaPlex–IMTalker pipeline into a real-time system on Jetson AGX Orin. Service time, GPU time and the audio and video delay actually delivered to a client are distinct quantities and were kept apart.

A major team effort. I contributed to the integration and system-development work; the delivered system reflects substantial contributions from the team.

Retrieval and tool use in the conversation

Giving an avatar access to outside information is a scheduling problem as much as a retrieval one: a lookup that arrives after the speech model has committed to a turn is of no use.

RAG and tool calling for conversational avatars

Retrieval, external tool access, conditioning of the generated turn and live speech scheduling, kept as separate concerns so that each could be reasoned about on its own.

A major team effort. I participated in this workstream; retrieval, tool access and live scheduling were developed collaboratively.
