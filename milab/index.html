MILab applied projects

Six strands of applied research in conversational avatars, from serving delay and audio-driven 3D faces to embedded execution.

Research Assistant (full-time) · North South University · March 2025–present

At MILab I work on conversational avatars, from a slow server to a real-time system on an embedded device. I had primary technical responsibility for the pipeline, representation and bridge work. The real-time system on Jetson AGX Orin and the retrieval and tool work were done with the team. Each section says what I did and what came of it.

Conversational pipelines and response delay

A listener notices how long they wait before they notice image quality. I built the serving path, with speech synthesis, generation and playback queues, and then worked on the delay it produced. The estimated end-to-end response delay fell from about 30 seconds to about 4 seconds.

I had primary technical responsibility for the pipeline and latency work. The robot deployment was a team effort.

MuseTalk 512×512 training and high-resolution development

I trained MuseTalk at 512×512 and developed the high-resolution avatar on top of it, raising output resolution while keeping the generation step inside the pipeline’s timing budget.

MuseTalk/LatentSync conversational pipeline and multi-GPU serving

Chunked execution, persistent text-to-speech, separate speech and video queues, and local and WebRTC playback paths, followed by a multi-GPU server.

The first version took roughly 30 seconds to respond, measured end to end from the moment the user finished speaking. The multi-GPU server brought that to about 4 seconds. Both are development estimates for that server.

I had primary technical responsibility for substantial parts of the integration and latency work.

Streaming avatar on a humanoid robot face

We took the streaming avatar off the screen and onto a physical robot head built by our industry partner, RB Labs. Their Robert humanoid robot provides the hardware and the surrounding product system. Our side was the face: driving the rendered head from streaming speech and fitting it to the robot’s display and timing constraints.

A team effort with an industry partner. My part was on the avatar generation and streaming side.

Audio-driven 3D avatars and blendshape control

Before the Gaussian and renderer work, our 3D avatars were driven through NVIDIA's Audio2Face. That route gives per-frame vertex motion for a face mesh, but a character rig is controlled by a small set of named blendshape channels, so the animation has to be recovered in those terms before an avatar can use it. Audio2Face is NVIDIA's software. I had primary technical responsibility for this strand.

Audio2Face-driven 3D avatars in the browser

I integrated a pre-trained Audio2Face model with a Three.js viewer so that generated speech could drive a 3D head in a browser session. The difficulty was in the plumbing, not in the model: model formats and texturing, converting and mapping the vertex animation onto the delivered mesh, and keeping playback aligned with the audio that produced it. A visible delay between audio and animation remained in this early integration.

Recovering blendshape controls from vertex animation

The network emits per-frame vertex offsets for the whole face mesh; what a rig needs is a weight for each of 52 standard blendshape controls. I framed the recovery as a regularised inverse problem: find the weights whose blendshape combination best reconstructs the frame’s vertex offsets. I solved it frame by frame with an iterative shrinkage-thresholding scheme, with the step size derived from the system matrix so that the solve stayed numerically stable.

The pipeline ran end to end and wrote control tracks in the target format, but the recovered weights came out heavily damped against the reference output. My explanation at the time was that the configuration’s regularisation strengths are tuned for the vendor’s own solver and do not transfer directly to this one. I did not get to test that.

Face representation and motion stability

A speech-driven avatar can be perfectly in time and still look wrong. I asked which representation and fitting choices keep facial motion stable. Several of these attempts did not work, and I describe them as they went.

I had primary technical responsibility for these investigations.

Head-wobble stabilization investigations

I ran controlled training and rendering variants to isolate unwanted head motion in speech-driven Gaussian avatars. The diagnostics describe training behaviour. I did not run a perceptual study of stability.

Pixel3DMM fitting and stabilization

I used Pixel3DMM as the fitting foundation to examine how face representation and motion estimation affect stability. The fitting path gave me a starting point for that comparison. It did not give a general fix.

I had primary technical responsibility for the fitting and representation pathway.

Custom FLAME articulation

I integrated FLAME and ran controlled articulation studies that separated fitting, motion and rendering, so that each could be varied on its own. One articulation-supervision proposal from this line was stopped on review. The wider FLAME and avatar work continued.

I had primary technical responsibility for this early articulation work.

Speech-to-motion bridges and renderer cost

A streaming speech model and a face renderer work in different natural units. Each pairing has to agree on chunk boundaries, buffering and what a late chunk means, so each bridge was its own piece of work. In parallel I measured what the renderer itself costs.

I had primary technical responsibility for these bridges and for the distillation study.

Gaussian avatar–Moshi integration

Moshi/Mimi speech representations connected to a GaussianTalker-based avatar, with streaming execution across the two components.

I had primary technical responsibility for the integration and streaming development.

Moshi–MuseTalk integration

Streaming adapters and execution infrastructure between conversational speech-model output and MuseTalk face generation, with attention to chunk boundaries and buffering.

IMTalker renderer distillation

I developed a fixed-identity teacher–student renderer and designed component-level benchmarks that separate renderer cost from end-to-end response time. Once the renderer was faster, the dominant cost moved upstream to speech generation, so a faster renderer alone did not mean a faster conversation.

I had primary technical responsibility for the renderer work and benchmark design.

Embedded real-time execution on Jetson AGX Orin

Moving the pipeline onto a single embedded device changes which costs matter. Export and precision decisions that are free on a server become the constraint, and the system has to hold a conversation within one power budget.

Jetson AGX Orin export and TensorRT optimization

I wrote the export tooling, integrated the engines, ran precision and graph checks, and profiled each component on the device. The timings belong to the hardware and power mode they were taken in, so I do not quote them here.

I had primary technical responsibility for the export and optimization paths.

PersonaPlex–IMTalker real-time customization on Orin

The team customized the PersonaPlex–IMTalker pipeline into a real-time system on Jetson AGX Orin. We measured service time, GPU time and the audio and video delay delivered to a client as separate quantities.

A major team effort. I contributed to the integration and system-development work.

Retrieval and tool use in the conversation

Giving an avatar access to outside information is a scheduling problem as much as a retrieval one: a lookup that arrives after the speech model has committed to a turn is of no use.

RAG and tool calling for conversational avatars

Retrieval, external tool access, conditioning of the generated turn and live speech scheduling, each built as a separate part.

A major team effort. I took part in this workstream; retrieval, tool access and live scheduling were developed together.
