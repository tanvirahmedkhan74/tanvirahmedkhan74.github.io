Self-supervised learning · Study note 4 of 5

From self-supervision to JEPA world models

A ball rolls behind a wall. What should a model be asked to predict about it, and what does that choice change?

Published 2026-11-13 · last reviewed 2026-11-13 · about 32 minutes · references

Assumes Vectors, dot products, softmax, Rank and what a linear map throws away

A ball rolls behind an occluder. With no label attached, what can a model learn from that video? This note follows one answer after another to that question: contrast two views of the same moment against other moments, predict a teacher's output, fill in missing pixels, or predict a representation of what is missing. It then separates three things that are easy to blur together: predicting a future, planning with a predictor, and generating video.

The methods are laid out as branches of a map, not as a staircase in which each step needs the last. The note ends with the recent Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) line, V-JEPA 2 and its action-conditioned variant V-JEPA 2-AC, then LeJEPA and LeWorldModel, with the evidence status of each claim stated: preprint, official technical report, or independently reproduced.

This is an educational note. All numerical examples are synthetic examples made for the note and computed in code; they are not results from the cited papers and not training runs.

The scene that runs through this note

Picture a short video. A ball rolls from the left, passes behind a grey occluder, and comes out on the right. In the middle frames the ball is not visible at all. Nobody has written a caption, a class name or a bounding box for any frame.

Two questions come up again and again. First, what replaces the label when there is none? Second, once the model has some way of learning, what stops it from learning something useless, such as outputting the same vector for every frame? That second failure is called collapse, and a large part of the history below is a history of different ways to avoid it.

Definition

Embedding (also latent or representation): a vector zz that an encoder ff computes from an input xx, written z=f(x)z = f(x). Self-supervised learning: training ff on a task whose answer is derived from the data itself, with no human labels. Collapse: the encoder maps many different inputs to the same, or nearly the same, embedding, so the embedding no longer tells inputs apart.

Learning without labels: what replaces the label

A supervised model is told, for each input, the right answer. A self-supervised model has to manufacture an answer from the input. The methods in this note differ mainly in which answer they manufacture:

  • Which views belong together. Take two crops or two nearby frames of the same scene. The 'label' is that they come from the same source and everything else does not (contrastive learning).
  • What a slowly changing copy of the model says. The 'label' is the output of a teacher network that is itself a smoothed copy of the student (BYOL, DINO).
  • What was hidden. The 'label' is the missing part of the input, either as raw pixels (masked autoencoders) or as a representation of those pixels (JEPA).
  • What the embeddings should look like as a population. A regulariser pushes a batch of embeddings towards a target spread (VICReg, SIGReg).

These are branches. A later method often borrows a part of an earlier one (a masking scheme, a teacher network, a loss term) without needing the earlier method to be understood first, and several threads continue in parallel today.

Contrastive learning and the InfoNCE objective

In our scene, take two frames a moment apart. They show the same ball in nearly the same place, so a good embedding should put them close together. Frames from a different video, with a different ball somewhere else, should land far away. The pair that belongs together is a positive. Every other frame in the batch is a negative. The signal that replaces the label is simply 'these two are the same moment'.

The standard way to turn this into a loss comes from Contrastive Predictive Coding (van den Oord et al., 2018), whose loss they call InfoNCE. In the notation commonly used by later image methods, take an anchor embedding qq, one positive embedding k0k_0 and KK negative embeddings k1,…,kKk_1,\dots,k_K:

ℒInfoNCE=−log⁡exp⁡(s(q,k0)/τ)∑j=0Kexp⁡(s(q,kj)/τ)\mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\log \frac{\exp\!\big(s(q,k_0)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{j=0}^{K}\exp\!\big(s(q,k_j)/\tau\big)}
  • s(q,k)s(q,k) is a similarity score, usually the dot product of two unit-length vectors (cosine similarity). Larger means more alike.
  • τ>0\tau>0 is the temperature. Dividing by a small τ\tau makes the softmax sharper, so a small similarity gap is turned into a large probability gap.
  • exp⁡(⋅)\exp(\cdot) makes every score positive, so each term can be read as an unnormalised probability.
  • The numerator is the positive's score. The denominator adds the positive and all KK negatives, so the ratio is the softmax probability of picking the positive out of K+1K+1 candidates.
  • The leading −log-\log turns that probability into a loss: it is zero when the positive gets probability 1 and grows as the probability falls.

So InfoNCE is a (K+1)(K+1)-way classification problem whose correct class is always 'the positive'. The CPC paper also shows that, with N=K+1N=K+1 candidates, the mutual information between the context and the target is bounded below by log⁡N\log N minus the loss, a relation that tightens as NN grows (CPC, Sec. 2.3). Two consequences follow, and both are checked numerically below.

Worked example

One anchor, one positive, three negatives. Suppose the four similarity scores are (0.9,0.1,0.0,−0.2)(0.9,\,0.1,\,0.0,\,-0.2) with the positive first, and τ=0.5\tau=0.5.

  1. Divide by τ\tau: (1.8,0.2,0,−0.4)(1.8,\,0.2,\,0,\,-0.4).
  2. Exponentiate: (6.0496,1.2214,1.0000,0.6703)(6.0496,\,1.2214,\,1.0000,\,0.6703), which sum to 8.94138.9413.
  3. The positive's probability is 6.0496/8.9413=0.67666.0496/8.9413 = 0.6766.
  4. The loss is −ln⁡0.6766=0.3907-\ln 0.6766 = 0.3907. At τ=1\tau=1 the loss is 0.78330.7833 and at τ=0.1\tau=0.1 it is 0.00050.0005; picking at random among four candidates would give ln⁡4=1.3863\ln 4 = 1.3863.

Consequence 1: a collapsed encoder scores exactly chance. If the encoder sends every input to the same unit vector, every score is 11, every softmax probability is 1/N1/N, and the loss is ln⁡N\ln N whatever the temperature. Computed: N=8N=8 gives 2.07942.0794, N=256N=256 gives 5.54525.5452, N=4096N=4096 gives 8.31788.3178, each equal to ln⁡N\ln N and each giving a mutual-information bound of zero. The gap ln⁡N−ℒ\ln N-\mathcal L in the table below is that bound: a batch estimate of how many nats of information about the positive the scores carry, which is zero exactly when the encoder does no better than guessing. This is why negatives prevent collapse in this family: the collapsed solution scores no better than chance, so it stops being a minimum as soon as any encoder does better. It is not the worst attainable loss — a predictor that ranks the positive below the negatives scores far worse (with τ=0.1\tau=0.1 and N=8N=8, an anti-aligned positive among aligned negatives costs 21.9521.95).

Consequence 2: the number of negatives matters. The ceiling on what the bound can certify is ln⁡N\ln N, so more candidates allow a tighter statement and force the encoder to separate more moments. The next section is about how methods get many negatives cheaply.

Where the negatives come from: memory banks, MoCo and SimCLR

InfoNCE wants many negatives, and negatives mean encoding many other frames. Two practical answers appeared close together.

Remember old embeddings. A memory bank stores embeddings from earlier steps and reuses them as negatives, so each step does not have to re-encode them. The cost is staleness: the stored vectors came from an older encoder. MoCo (He et al., 2019/2020) treats the negatives as a dictionary held in a queue, refreshed first-in first-out, and keeps the keys consistent by encoding them with a momentum encoder, a slowly moving average of the query encoder, θk←mθk+(1−m)θq\theta_k \leftarrow m\,\theta_k + (1-m)\,\theta_q. The paper's main setting uses a queue of K=65536K=65536 and m=0.999m=0.999.

Use a large batch. SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020) drops the memory bank and takes the other items in the same batch as negatives. For a batch of NN images there are 2N2N augmented views, so each positive pair is contrasted against 2(N−1)2(N-1) others; the paper trains with batch sizes up to 8192, which gives 16382 negatives per positive pair. Its loss, called NT-Xent, is the InfoNCE form above applied symmetrically to both members of every pair. A small check: with N=4N=4 pairs, so 8 views and 7 candidates per anchor, τ=0.5\tau=0.5 and the ring encoder above, the loss is 0.50850.5085 against chance ln⁡7=1.9459\ln 7 = 1.9459.

The trade is memory versus consistency versus hardware. A queue decouples the number of negatives from the batch size; a large batch keeps everything fresh but needs large accelerators. In our running scene, either way, the negatives are other moments and other videos, and they are what stops the encoder from deciding that all frames look alike.

Caution

A negative is only 'wrong' by assumption. A frame from a different clip of the same ball rolling behind the same occluder is labelled a negative even though it is semantically almost identical. Contrastive methods pay for this with a push-apart force on genuinely similar items.

Dropping the negatives: BYOL, DINO and why collapse is the worry

Now remove the negatives and keep only the invariance part: two views of the same moment should have close embeddings. The trouble is that this is satisfied perfectly by an encoder that ignores its input. A synthetic check: for two views of an angle 0.20.2 apart, the informative ring encoder pays a mean squared distance of 0.039870.03987, and a constant encoder pays 0.000000.00000. The constant wins. The BYOL paper states this directly: the objective admits collapsed solutions such as outputting the same vector for all images.

BYOL (Grill et al., 2020) trains an online network to predict the output of a target network, where the target is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online weights, the online side has an extra predictor head, and gradients are stopped through the target. It reports 74.3% top-1 ImageNet linear accuracy with a ResNet-50 and uses no negative pairs. Its authors are careful about the explanation. They write that they hypothesise there is no loss that this dynamic descends jointly, that undesirable equilibria still exist, and that they did not observe convergence to them in their experiments (BYOL, Sec. 3.2). That is an empirical observation about a training recipe, not a guarantee.

DINO (Caron et al., 2021) is likewise a student and a momentum teacher, with the student matching the teacher's output distribution over a set of prototypes. It avoids collapse with two opposing operations on the teacher's output: centering, which stops one dimension from dominating but pushes towards a uniform output, and sharpening, which has the opposite effect. The paper reports that the two together suffice, in the presence of a momentum teacher, to avoid collapse.

Notice what these two have in common: the protection against collapse is a property of the training dynamics (EMA, stop-gradient, predictor, centering). Nothing in the loss itself forbids the constant solution. That is the point to remember when the later regularisation methods appear: they move the protection from the dynamics into an explicit term that measures the embedding population.

Masked pixel reconstruction, and what it spends capacity on

A different manufactured answer is the input itself. Masked autoencoders (MAE; He et al., 2021) hide a large random fraction of image patches (75% in their default), encode only the visible patches, and train a light decoder to reconstruct the hidden pixels. The paper reports that using per-patch normalised pixels as the target improves representation quality. There is no collapse problem to solve: a constant output cannot reconstruct a varied image, so the target itself forces variety.

The question this section is about is subtler: when the target is pixels, what must the model get right to score well? In our scene the answer includes the ball, and also the exact grain of the wall behind it, the lighting gradient, and any sensor noise. Some of those are predictable; some are not predictable from anything visible at all.

Here is a synthetic example made for the note, computed in code. Take a strip of 16 pixels with a bright bar of width 3 at a random position, add independent Gaussian texture of standard deviation 0.5 to every pixel, and hide pixels 5 to 10 behind an occluder. Even an oracle that knows the bar's position exactly cannot predict the texture, so its best pixel mean-squared error summed over the hidden columns is 6×0.52=1.5006\times 0.5^{2} = 1.500. The variance of the hidden pixels about their per-column means is 2.5072.507, which is what a predictor that knows nothing about the bar would pay. So 59.9%59.9\% of that error is texture that no knowledge of the bar's position can remove, and 1.5001.500 is a floor for every predictor, however good. A target that records only the bar's position (one number) has no such floor.

Caution

This toy shows a mechanism, not a measurement of MAE. Real images have structure that a reconstruction decoder can partly exploit, and MAE's reported transfer results are strong. The claim is narrow: a pixel loss weights every pixel, so unpredictable detail contributes to the loss whether or not it matters for the task.

I-JEPA: predicting in latent space instead of pixel space

The Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture idea keeps the 'predict what was hidden' task and changes the thing predicted. I-JEPA (Assran et al., CVPR 2023) encodes a visible context block, and a narrow predictor, told only the positions of the target blocks, outputs a representation for each. The targets are not pixels. They are the representations that a separate target encoder computes for the target blocks, where the target encoder is an EMA of the context encoder and carries no gradient. The loss is the average distance between predicted and target patch representations (an L2L_2 distance in I-JEPA). The paper samples four target blocks per image and uses no hand-crafted augmentations.

Why does this change what the model must represent? A pixel target asks the model to emit every detail of the hidden region. A latent target asks it to emit whatever the target encoder retained. If the target encoder has learned to keep 'where is the ball, how fast is it moving' and to drop grain, then predicting its output requires the context encoder to carry exactly that. Two honest qualifications keep this from becoming a slogan. First, the target encoder is itself being learned, so what it keeps is a result of training, not a design guarantee. Second, a latent target is only useful if it has not collapsed, which is why EMA targets and stop-gradients appear in this family.

The latent view also changes what 'uncertainty' means in the running scene. When the ball is hidden, several states are consistent with the visible frame: it may keep going, stop, or reverse. A pixel predictor trained with a squared-error loss tends towards an average of those possibilities, a blur. A latent predictor can output a coarser description that is the same across the possibilities, such as 'the ball is behind the occluder', and so avoids committing to a specific rendering. Whether a given trained model actually behaves this way is an empirical question for each model.

Caution

An intuition pump, not evidence. A random-dot stereogram is a pair of images that each look like pure noise, yet shifting the dots between them produces a clear sense of depth. It is a handy picture of an observation whose information lives in the relation between parts, not in any single part, which is also the situation when the hidden region cannot be recovered pixel by pixel. It is not evidence about how JEPA works internally, and it is not evidence about how any brain works. No result in this note rests on it.

V-JEPA, V-JEPA 2 and V-JEPA 2-AC

Moving from images to video turns the hidden block into a hidden spatio-temporal region, which brings us back to the ball.

V-JEPA (Bardes et al., 2024) masks spatio-temporal regions of a video and predicts their feature representations, with a stop-gradient and an EMA target encoder and an L1L_1 loss. The abstract reports training on 2 million public videos with no pretrained image encoder, text, negatives or pixel reconstruction, and a ViT-H/16 reaching 81.9% on Kinetics-400, 72.2% on Something-Something-v2 and 77.9% on ImageNet-1K with a frozen backbone. These are the authors' reported numbers on their chosen benchmarks.

V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., Meta FAIR, 2025) keeps the mask-denoising feature-prediction objective, written in the paper as min⁡‖Pϕ(Δy,Eθ(x))−sg(Eθ‾(y))‖1\min \lVert P_\phi(\Delta_y, E_\theta(x)) - \mathrm{sg}(E_{\bar\theta}(y))\rVert_1: a predictor PϕP_\phi fills in learnable mask tokens Δy\Delta_y from the visible video xx, and the target is the stop-gradient output of an EMA encoder on the full video yy. The paper states plainly that this stop-gradient and EMA are there to prevent representation collapse. Pretraining uses a curated mix called VideoMix22M (22 million samples) and, per the abstract, over one million hours of video and images, with no actions. Reported results include 77.3 top-1 on Something-Something v2 and 39.7 recall-at-5 on Epic-Kitchens-100 action anticipation, and, after alignment with a language model at the 8-billion-parameter scale, 84.0 on PerceptionTest and 76.9 on TempCompass.

V-JEPA 2-AC is a second stage. The pretrained encoder is frozen, and a new action-conditioned predictor is trained on under 62 hours of unlabelled robot video from the DROID dataset (about 23 thousand trajectories, successes and failures). At each step the predictor receives the current frame's representation, the robot's end-effector state and the commanded action, and predicts the next frame's representation. It is trained with a teacher-forcing loss and a two-step rollout loss, both L1L_1 in representation space. The model's own paper then deploys it without further training on Franka arms in two labs.

Caution

Two scope limits worth keeping. First, V-JEPA 2 pretraining is action-free: it sees video, not the commands that produced it. Only the small second stage is action-conditioned, and only that stage can be asked 'what happens if I do this?'. Second, the robot results come from the authors' own deployments, with 10 trials per skill in each table row. A reported 60% means 6 of 10, a sample too small to separate close methods, and the paper itself notes sensitivity to camera position and difficulty with long horizons.

Prediction is not planning

It is easy to say 'a good world model can plan' and slide from one skill to the other. They are different computations, trained and evaluated differently.

Prediction and planning separated, following the V-JEPA 2-AC description.
PredictionPlanning
Question answeredGiven the current state and an action, what will the latent state be?Which action sequence leads to a state close to the goal?
What is learnedA predictor PP, by regression on observed transitionsNothing new in the model: a search over actions using PP
Needs a goalNoYes, here an image of the goal, encoded by the same encoder
Needs a costNo, only a prediction errorYes, here the L1L_1 energy ‖ẑt+T−zg‖1\lVert \hat z_{t+T} - z_g\rVert_1
Typical failureErrors compound along a long rolloutA poor energy landscape, a small search budget, or a goal the encoder cannot express
How it is scoredPrediction error, probesTask success when a robot or simulator actually executes the plan

In V-JEPA 2-AC, planning is model predictive control: optimise a sequence of actions over a short horizon by minimising the energy, execute only the first action, observe, and plan again. The paper optimises with the cross-entropy method, a sampling search. A better predictor makes a better search possible, but does not by itself guarantee one. The paper reports looking at the energy landscape for a reaching task and finding it relatively smooth and locally convex, which is a check on that one task, not a general property.

Video generation is a third thing. A model that renders plausible next frames can be useful to look at, and an action-conditioned video generator can be dropped into the same planning loop as a world model. The V-JEPA 2 paper does exactly this with Cosmos, an action-conditioned video-generation model, in its comparison table: with its settings, Cosmos took about 4 minutes per action against about 16 seconds for V-JEPA 2-AC, and scored lower on object-interaction tasks. Two honest readings of that row: it supports the idea that planning in a compact latent space can be cheaper, and it is also a single comparison from the authors of one of the two systems, under a particular budget (80 versus 800 samples per refinement step). It does not show that generating video is unnecessary or that a latent model is better in general.

Collapse prevention across this family

Every thread above had to answer the collapse question, and the answers fall into a few kinds.

Collapse prevention grouped by mechanism, from the papers' own descriptions.
MechanismWhere it appearsWhat stops the constant solution
NegativesCPC, MoCo, SimCLRThe loss at a constant encoder is exactly ln⁡N\ln N: chance level, so any encoder that beats chance escapes it
Teacher asymmetryBYOL, I-JEPA, V-JEPA, V-JEPA 2EMA target, stop-gradient and a predictor; an empirical recipe rather than a derived guarantee

The last row is the subject of the companion note on collapse diagnostics, which defines complete and dimensional collapse, shows how to measure them and how those measurements can mislead, and reads the VICReg, SIGReg and VISReg objectives from their papers. The short version for this note: an explicit population regulariser is a different kind of protection from a teacher-student recipe, and the two kinds are not interchangeable evidence of each other.

One detail is easy to miss. V-JEPA 2 (June 2025) still relies on stop-gradient and EMA for collapse prevention, as its own text says; whether V-JEPA 2.1 (March 2026) keeps both was not checked for this note. The regulariser-based line (LeJEPA, LeWorldModel) reports training without them. These are different systems on different data, so neither side's results show that the other mechanism is unnecessary.

LeJEPA, LeWorldModel and what came after

LeJEPA (Balestriero and LeCun, arXiv November 2025, a preprint) argues, within its own analysis, that the embedding distribution which minimises downstream prediction risk is an isotropic Gaussian, and proposes a regulariser, Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularisation (SIGReg), to push the embeddings of a batch towards it. Its total loss is a single trade-off between a prediction term and SIGReg:

ℒLeJEPA=λV∑v=1VSIGReg({zn,v}n=1B)+1−λB∑n=1Bℒpred\mathcal{L}_{\text{LeJEPA}} = \frac{\lambda}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\mathrm{SIGReg}\big(\{z_{n,v}\}_{n=1}^{B}\big) + \frac{1-\lambda}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{B}\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}}

Here BB is the batch size, VV the number of views and λ\lambda the one trade-off hyperparameter. The paper states that the recipe needs no stop-gradient, no teacher-student pair and no hyper-parameter schedulers, and reports 79% ImageNet-1k linear-probe accuracy with a ViT-H/14, an author-reported figure. What SIGReg tests exactly is covered in the companion note.

LeWorldModel (LeWM; Maes et al., arXiv March 2026, latest version June 2026, a preprint) applies this to an action-conditioned world model that learns from pixels. Its objective has two terms, ℒpred+λSIGReg(Z)\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} + \lambda\,\mathrm{SIGReg}(Z) with λ=0.1\lambda=0.1 and 1024 projections by default, where ℒpred\mathcal{L}_{\text{pred}} is next-embedding prediction. The reported model has about 15 million parameters, trains on one GPU in a few hours, and plans with the cross-entropy method roughly 48 times faster than a foundation-model-based baseline (about one second against 47 seconds per plan on the authors' setup). The authors also publish limits: LeWM underperforms on the low-dimensional Two-Room task, they suspect because the Gaussian regulariser struggles when the task's intrinsic dimension is small; the baseline DINO-WM leads on the visually complex OGBench-Cube task; and on one Push-T probe the baseline decodes block angle better (mean squared error 0.187 for LeWM against 0.050).

Caution

LeWorldModel is a recent preprint with its own limitations, not an endpoint. It is one point on one branch of the map: a compact, regulariser-based world model evaluated on a handful of small control tasks. It does not settle which collapse-prevention mechanism is best, it does not show that latent world models scale to open-ended real-world settings, and it says nothing of the kind about any general-intelligence claim. Its own text lists short planning horizons as a limitation.

A small verified update. As of the check on 13 November 2026, two later items are on the record. V-JEPA 2.1 (Mur-Labadia et al., arXiv March 2026, a preprint with three versions) reports dense features from a loss on both visible and masked tokens, and states a 20-point gain in real-robot grasping success over V-JEPA 2-AC; this is the authors' own comparison. The V-JEPA 2 code repository lists the V-JEPA 2.1 release. And VISReg (Wu, Balestriero and Levine, arXiv June 2026, a preprint) proposes a different regulariser; the companion note reads its objective. This is not a survey of 2026 world-model work, only the items checked here.

How much each claim should be trusted

Three kinds of source appear in this note, and they support different amounts. A preprint is a paper posted to arXiv; the page used for this note did not show peer review for the papers marked so below. An official technical report is a document from the team that built and released the system, which is informative about what they did but is their own account. An independent reproduction is a result re-obtained by people not involved, with their own code or data. For the claims below, no independent reproduction was located at the date of checking.

Two further distinctions deserve a sentence each. A probing result shows that a small model, trained on labelled examples, can read a quantity out of a frozen representation. That is real evidence that the information is present and easily accessible. It is not evidence that the model understands physics: a probe can succeed on correlated cues, and a model can encode a quantity without being able to use it to predict interventions. The LeWM paper pairs its probes with surprise tests on physically implausible events; such tests add to the picture, and still sit well short of showing causal understanding. And action-free video learning is a different claim from action-conditioned control, as the V-JEPA 2 structure itself shows.
