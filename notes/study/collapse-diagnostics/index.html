Measuring collapse: spectra, effective rank, VICReg, SIGReg and VISReg

A table of embeddings can look healthy on one measurement and be broken on another. How to measure it, how each measurement can lie, and what three recent objectives actually ask for.

Published 2026-11-13 · last reviewed 2026-11-13 · about 34 minutes · references

Assumes Mean, variance, covariance, eigenvalues and the singular value decomposition, Rank and what a linear map throws away, Why collapse matters in this family

A model turns 512 images into 512 vectors of 8 numbers each. Is that table useful, or has the model quietly mapped everything to one point, or to a thin sliver of the space? This note builds the measurements one at a time on small synthetic tables made for the purpose: per-dimension spread, the centred covariance matrix, the correlation matrix, the eigenvalue spectrum, and one precisely defined effective rank.

It then shows, with numbers computed in code, how each measurement can mislead: through scale, through too few samples, through forgetting to centre, through standardising, and through thresholds near zero. Finally it reads three objectives from their own papers (VICReg's variance, invariance and covariance terms; LeJEPA's SIGReg; VISReg's scale, shape and centre terms) and compares what each one states it constrains.

Three distinctions run through the note and are made explicit where they matter: decorrelation is not independence; matching a Gaussian is not the same as being semantically useful; and nonzero variance does not rule out dimensional collapse. Every point cloud here is a teaching illustration, not a training trajectory, and no trained methods are compared.

Two ways a table of embeddings can collapse

Take 512 inputs and an encoder that summarises each as a vector of 8 numbers. Stack the vectors as the rows of a table ZZ with n=512n=512 rows and d=8d=8 columns. Each row is an embedding. In a good table, different inputs have visibly different rows and the 8 columns carry different information.

There are two broad ways this goes wrong. In complete collapse, every row is (nearly) the same vector, so the table carries no information about which input produced it. In dimensional collapse (also called partial collapse), rows do differ, but only along a few directions: the cloud of 512 points in 8 dimensions is really a flat sheet, or a line, with the remaining directions unused. Self-supervised objectives that compare views of the same input are vulnerable to both, because a constant output makes two views trivially agree; the VICReg authors state that such methods can collapse to constant outputs (Bardes, Ponce and LeCun, 2022).

Here are four synthetic tables, each 512×8512\times 8 and generated in code with fixed seeds for this note. The first is a healthy isotropic Gaussian cloud. The second is complete collapse: one point (every coordinate near 3) plus jitter of standard deviation 0.001. The third is dimensional collapse: a rank-2 cloud rotated into 8 dimensions, with every coordinate then rescaled to standard deviation 1. The fourth is a cloud whose columns come in pairs (u,v)(u, v) with v=(u2−1)/2v=(u^{2}-1)/\sqrt 2, built to be uncorrelated yet dependent. Figure 1 shows two-dimensional versions so the geometry is visible.

The table already contains the note's main warning. No single row of the figure separates all four clouds: standard deviation misses the line, correlation misses the parabola, and effective rank misses the point. Each measurement is a window onto one property, and the sections below say exactly which.

The measurements: spread, covariance, correlation, spectrum

Write the rows of ZZ as z1,…,zn∈ℝdz_1,\dots,z_n\in\mathbb{R}^d and let z‾=1n∑izi\bar z=\tfrac1n\sum_i z_i be their mean. Four standard summaries follow.

  • Per-dimension spread. The standard deviation of each column jj over the batch, std⁡(zj)\operatorname{std}(z^{j}). It answers 'does this coordinate vary at all?' and nothing else.
  • The centred covariance matrix, a d×dd\times d matrix whose (j,k)(j,k) entry says how coordinates jj and kk vary together after the mean has been removed (formula below).
  • The correlation matrix, which divides each covariance entry by the product of the two standard deviations, so that every entry lies between −1-1 and 11 and the diagonal is 1.
  • The spectrum: the eigenvalues λ1≥…≥λd\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_d of the covariance matrix, which measure how much variance lies along each principal direction.
C(Z)=1n−1∑i=1n(zi−z‾)(zi−z‾)⊤,Rjk=CjkCjjCkkC(Z)=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(z_i-\bar z)(z_i-\bar z)^{\top},\qquad R_{jk}=\frac{C_{jk}}{\sqrt{C_{jj}\,C_{kk}}}

The covariance formula is Eq. (3) of the VICReg paper. Two details matter later. Centring (subtracting z‾\bar z) is part of the definition: without it the matrix mixes 'where the cloud sits' with 'how it spreads'. And the diagonal of CC holds the per-dimension variances, so per-dimension spread is a view of the diagonal only, while collapse is often a statement about the off-diagonal structure.

The eigenvalues connect to the singular values σk\sigma_k of the centred table ZcZ_c (the table with z‾\bar z subtracted from every row) by λk=σk2/(n−1)\lambda_k=\sigma_k^{2}/(n-1); the code checks this to 10−1610^{-16}. The trace of CC, the sum of its eigenvalues, equals the total variance.

Caution

Nonzero variance does not rule out dimensional collapse. In the third table every one of the 8 coordinates has standard deviation 1.001, comfortably above any 'is this column dead?' threshold, and the table is still essentially two-dimensional: six of the eight covariance eigenvalues are below 6×10−66\times10^{-6}. Rotating or mixing coordinates changes which columns look alive without changing how many directions are used. Only the off-diagonal structure, or the spectrum, shows it.

Effective rank: one definition, used throughout

The ordinary rank of a matrix counts nonzero singular values, which is an all-or-nothing integer: a table with one tiny nonzero singular value has full rank. A diagnostic that is useful for training needs a smooth number. This note uses a single definition throughout, the effective rank of Roy and Vetterli (EUSIPCO 2007).

Definition

Let AA be a nonzero matrix with singular values σ1≥…≥σQ≥0\sigma_1\ge\dots\ge\sigma_Q\ge0, Q=min⁡{rows,columns}Q=\min\{\text{rows},\text{columns}\}. Define the distribution pk=σk/∑jσjp_k=\sigma_k/\sum_j\sigma_j (the singular values divided by their sum, an ℓ1\ell_1 normalisation). The effective rank is the exponential of its Shannon entropy, with the convention 0log⁡0=00\log0=0:

erank⁡(A)=exp⁡(−∑k=1Qpkln⁡pk),pk=σk∑jσj\operatorname{erank}(A)=\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{k=1}^{Q}p_k\ln p_k\Big),\qquad p_k=\frac{\sigma_k}{\sum_{j}\sigma_j}

Properties stated in the paper: 1≤erank⁡(A)≤rank⁡(A)1\le\operatorname{erank}(A)\le\operatorname{rank}(A), with equality at 1 only if there is a single nonzero singular value, and the value does not change if AA is multiplied by a nonzero constant.

In this note AA is always the centred embedding table ZcZ_c (n×dn\times d), and the singular values are those of ZcZ_c, not eigenvalues of the covariance matrix. This choice matters: using the covariance eigenvalues λk=σk2/(n−1)\lambda_k=\sigma_k^2/(n-1) in the same formula squares the ratios and gives a smaller number, so values computed one way must never be compared with values computed the other. Everything below uses singular values.

Worked example

Two singular values (3,1)(3,1). Then p=(0.75,0.25)p=(0.75,0.25), the entropy is H=−0.75ln⁡0.75−0.25ln⁡0.25=0.5623H=-0.75\ln0.75-0.25\ln0.25=0.5623, and erank⁡=e0.5623=1.7548\operatorname{erank}=e^{0.5623}=1.7548. With (1,1)(1,1) the same recipe gives exactly 2, and with (1,0)(1,0) it gives exactly 1. So 1.75 reads as 'about one and three-quarters directions are really in use'.

Applied to the four tables of Figure 2 the effective rank is 7.98 (healthy), 7.98 (complete collapse), 2.02 (dimensional collapse) and 7.94 (decorrelated but dependent), out of a maximum of 8.

Look at the second value. The complete collapse has effective rank 7.98, almost the maximum. Its jitter is isotropic, so after centring, the leftover noise is spread evenly over all eight directions and the number reports 'many directions', although the whole cloud is a speck of width 0.001. The effective rank is scale-free by construction, which is a strength when measuring shape and a blindness when measuring size. The animation shows this directly with a two-dimensional cloud: first squeezed onto a line (effective rank falls from 2.00 to 1.14 while each coordinate still varies), then shrunk to a point (variance falls to 0.0009 while the effective rank does not move).

How a diagnostic lies

Each of the following is a way to get a confident, wrong answer from a correct formula. Every number below was computed in code written for this note.

Scale. Multiply the dimensional-collapse table by 10−310^{-3} and the effective rank is unchanged at 2.018, while the minimum per-coordinate standard deviation falls from 1.001 to 1.001×10−31.001\times10^{-3}. A rule such as 'a coordinate is alive if its standard deviation exceeds 0.01' now declares 0 of 8 coordinates alive; at scale 10310^{3} it declares all 8 alive. The rank is a statement about shape; the threshold is a statement about size. Report both, and report them with the scale of the embedding stated.

Sample size versus dimension. After centring, a table with nn rows has rank at most n−1n-1. So with too few samples the measurement is limited by the batch, not by the model. For an isotropic Gaussian in d=64d=64 dimensions, whose true effective dimension is as large as it can be, the measured effective rank is 6.86 for n=8n=8, 14.50 for n=16n=16, 51.08 for n=64n=64, 62.97 for n=512n=512 and 63.88 for n=4096n=4096. A reading of 14.5 is not evidence of collapse if the batch had 16 rows.

Centring. Take the healthy table and add 10 to every coordinate, a constant offset. Centred, the effective rank is 7.98. With centring skipped, the largest singular value is dominated by the offset and the number falls to 2.41. A healthy table looks collapsed because 'where the cloud is' was mistaken for 'how it spreads'. The mistake does not run the other way: skipping centring on the completely collapsed table gives 1.01, because the offset is then the only direction with any spread. An uncentred number can only be dragged towards the offset, never away from it.

Normalisation. Build a table where two coordinates have standard deviation 1 and six have standard deviation 10−310^{-3}. Raw, the effective rank is 2.05. Standardise every column to unit variance, as many pipelines do before computing a diagnostic, and the number becomes 7.99: the six dead columns have been blown up to the same scale as the live ones, and the collapse disappears from the measurement.

Thresholds near zero. Take a noiseless rank-2 table in 8 dimensions. In double precision its six trailing singular values are of order 10−1410^{-14} to 10−1610^{-16}, and in single precision of order 10−710^{-7}. Counting 'nonzero' singular values gives 8 in both precisions, for a table of rank 2. An absolute threshold of 10−610^{-6} gives 2 in both cases here, but if the table is scaled by 100 in single precision the leading tail value is 8.2×10−58.2\times10^{-5} and the same threshold counts 8. A relative threshold (10−310^{-3} times the largest singular value) gives 2 in every case tried. When all singular values are exactly zero, as for a constant table, the effective rank is 0/00/0 and must be guarded explicitly; the computation here returns not-a-number.

The noise floor decides the answer. Add independent noise of standard deviation ss to a rank-2 table: the effective rank is 1.9989 at s=0s=0, 2.0247 at s=10−3s=10^{-3}, 2.19 at s=10−2s=10^{-2}, 3.39 at s=0.1s=0.1 and 7.44 at s=1s=1. A table that is 'collapsed' to rank 2 plus a little noise gets a slowly increasing number, so a single threshold on effective rank is a choice about how much noise counts as signal.

Check it yourself

VICReg: three terms, read from the paper

VICReg (Bardes, Ponce and LeCun, ICLR 2022) takes two augmented views of each input, encodes each, and passes them through a small expander network to get two batches of embeddings Z=[z1,…,zn]Z=[z_1,\dots,z_n] and Z′=[z′1,…,z′n]Z'=[z'_1,\dots,z'_n], each of nn vectors in ℝd\mathbb{R}^d. Its loss has three terms, written here as in the paper's Eq. (1) to (6).

s(Z,Z′)=1n∑i=1n‖zi−z′i‖22s(Z,Z')=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert z_i-z'_i\rVert_2^{2}

The invariance term ss pulls the two views of the same input together. On its own it is minimised by a constant output.

v(Z)=1d∑j=1dmax⁡(0,γ−S(zj,ϵ)),S(x,ϵ)=Var⁡(x)+ϵv(Z)=\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^{d}\max\!\big(0,\;\gamma-S(z^{j},\epsilon)\big),\qquad S(x,\epsilon)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(x)+\epsilon}

The variance term is a hinge on the standard deviation of each coordinate zjz^{j} (the jj-th column of the batch). It is zero for any coordinate whose standard deviation is at least the target γ\gamma, and positive otherwise. The paper fixes γ=1\gamma=1 and ϵ=10−4\epsilon=10^{-4}. It uses the standard deviation and not the variance because, the paper explains, the gradient of the variance version approaches zero as the embeddings approach their mean, so the embeddings would collapse. The next sections check that claim numerically.

c(Z)=1d∑j≠k[C(Z)]jk2c(Z)=\frac1d\sum_{j\neq k}\big[C(Z)\big]_{jk}^{2}

The covariance term sums the squared off-diagonal entries of the covariance matrix defined earlier, with the 1/d1/d factor shown. It pushes different coordinates towards being uncorrelated. The full objective is a weighted sum applied to both branches:

ℓ(Z,Z′)=λs(Z,Z′)+μ[v(Z)+v(Z′)]+ν[c(Z)+c(Z′)]\ell(Z,Z')=\lambda\,s(Z,Z')+\mu\,[v(Z)+v(Z')]+\nu\,[c(Z)+c(Z')]

In the paper's ImageNet setup λ=μ=25\lambda=\mu=25 and ν=1\nu=1. In its coefficient study the authors report that without variance regularisation the representations collapse to a single vector, that the covariance term alone has no repulsive effect to prevent that, and that invariance plus variance alone reaches 57.5% accuracy, concluding that variance and covariance have complementary effects. These are the paper's own training results on ImageNet.

On the toy tables, with γ=1\gamma=1, ϵ=10−4\epsilon=10^{-4} and the second view made by adding noise of standard deviation 0.05 to every coordinate (so the invariance term is about 0.0200.020 in every case), the terms read as follows.

Caution

Decorrelation is not independence. The covariance term drives off-diagonal covariances to zero, which removes linear dependence only. In the fourth table every pair (u,v)(u,v) with v=(u2−1)/2v=(u^2-1)/\sqrt2 has correlation −0.046-0.046 (for the first pair), so the covariance term is small, 0.0819 against 0.0245 for the healthy table, yet vv is an exact function of uu: the correlation of u2u^{2} with vv is 1.0000. The 8-dimensional table lies on four parabolas, a 4-dimensional set, and covariance cannot see it. The LeJEPA and VISReg papers make the same point about second-order statistics: a distribution can match in mean and covariance and still be far from an isotropic Gaussian.

SIGReg: what the sketched constraint tests

LeJEPA (Balestriero and LeCun, arXiv 2025, a preprint) replaces the hand-picked variance and covariance terms by a single statistical test. Its argument, within its own analysis, is that the best embedding distribution for downstream prediction is an isotropic Gaussian, 𝒩(0,I)\mathcal N(0,I), and it asks the batch of embeddings to look like a sample from that distribution.

Testing a distribution in many dimensions directly is expensive, so SIGReg ('Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularisation') sketches the test. It draws MM random unit vectors aa, projects every embedding onto each (giving a one-dimensional list a⊤f(xn)a^{\top}f(x_n)), applies a univariate test of the hypothesis that this list is a sample from 𝒩(0,1)\mathcal N(0,1), and averages the MM test statistics. The justification is the Cramér-Wold theorem: a distribution on ℝd\mathbb R^d is determined by its one-dimensional projections, so if every projection matches 𝒩(0,1)\mathcal N(0,1), the embedding distribution is 𝒩(0,I)\mathcal N(0,I).

SIGRegT(𝒜,{fθ(xn)}n=1N)=1|𝒜|∑a∈𝒜T({a⊤fθ(xn)}n=1N)\mathrm{SIGReg}_T\big(\mathcal A,\{f_\theta(x_n)\}_{n=1}^{N}\big)=\frac1{|\mathcal A|}\sum_{a\in\mathcal A}T\big(\{a^{\top}f_\theta(x_n)\}_{n=1}^{N}\big)

The paper recommends the Epps-Pulley test for TT. It compares the empirical characteristic function of the projected sample, φ̂(t)=1N∑neitxn\hat\varphi(t)=\tfrac1N\sum_n e^{\,\mathrm i t x_n} (the average of complex exponentials of the sample), with the characteristic function of the target, φ(t)=e−t2/2\varphi(t)=e^{-t^{2}/2}, in a weighted squared distance:

T=N∫−∞∞|φ̂(t)−φ(t)|2w(t)dtT=N\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big|\hat\varphi(t)-\varphi(t)\big|^{2}\,w(t)\,dt

In the paper's Algorithm 1 the weight is w(t)=e−t2/2w(t)=e^{-t^{2}/2} (its text writes e−t2/σ2e^{-t^{2}/\sigma^{2}} with σ=1\sigma=1; this note follows the code, and the 1.0594 null average above is the value that choice implies) and the integral is approximated by the trapezoid rule on 17 knots from −5-5 to 55. The scripts for this note implement exactly that, and check the hand-derived gradient against finite differences (agreement 5×10−105\times10^{-10}).

Because the target is 𝒩(0,1)\mathcal N(0,1) in every direction, a single projection is tested for location (is its mean 0?), scale (is its variance 1?) and shape (is it bell-shaped, with the right tails?) at once. Figure 4 shows why random directions are needed: a column-by-column check, which is the direction a1a_1, passes. The check with the Epps-Pulley statistic on a single projection of 4096 points gives a small value for Gaussian data and a far larger one otherwise. The large-sample average of the statistic under the null is 1.0594 with this quadrature, but the null distribution is skewed and a single draw moves about: the one 𝒩(0,1)\mathcal N(0,1) draw recorded here (one direction, n=4096n=4096) is 3.27. Against that, for non-Gaussian data with the same mean and variance: 68.8 for a uniform sample and 73.1 for a Laplace sample, against 466 for a mean shifted by 0.5, 620 for a standard deviation of 0.5 and 1027 for a standard deviation of 2. On the toy tables (256 directions) the statistic is 1.03 (healthy), 46.2 (dimensional collapse), 14.3 (decorrelated, dependent) and 1281.6 (complete collapse, partly because the offset mean of 3 is penalised).

The whole objective combines this with a prediction term:

ℒLeJEPA=λV∑v=1VSIGReg({zn,v}n=1B)+1−λB∑n=1Bℒpred\mathcal L_{\text{LeJEPA}}=\frac{\lambda}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\mathrm{SIGReg}\big(\{z_{n,v}\}_{n=1}^{B}\big)+\frac{1-\lambda}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{B}\mathcal L_{\text{pred}}

with a single trade-off λ\lambda. The paper also remarks that choosing the simple statistic mean2+(std−1)2\text{mean}^2+(\text{std}-1)^2 for TT recovers VICReg in the limit of many slices; for that reason it recommends against it, and uses Epps-Pulley.

Caution

Matching a Gaussian is not the same as being semantically useful. Draw two independent embedding tables from 𝒩(0,I)\mathcal N(0,I) in 8 dimensions, 4000 rows each, and label every row by whether its first coordinate is positive. A least-squares linear probe fitted on the first table reaches 98.5% accuracy on the second. Now keep the identical embedding law but assign labels at random: the probe reaches 51.0%. The SIGReg statistic of the fitting table is 1.12 either way, because it depends only on the table and never on the labels. A perfectly Gaussian embedding can be semantically empty, and the objective cannot tell. Whether the distribution that SIGReg enforces is also useful for a given downstream task is something the LeJEPA paper argues theoretically and reports empirically; it is not something the statistic itself measures.

Caution

A finite number of directions MM and a finite sample NN mean the constraint is enforced approximately. At N=64N=64, 512512 and 40964096 points from a true isotropic Gaussian the statistic fluctuates between about 0.7 and 1.25 here, not 0. The paper also reports a bias of order 1/N1/N in the minibatch gradient.

VISReg: its own decomposition

VISReg (Wu, Balestriero and Levine, arXiv June 2026, a preprint) keeps VICReg's idea of controlling the scale of each coordinate, and replaces its covariance term with a sketched shape term. The paper describes the regulariser as decoupled into three parts. First a scale loss on the centred embeddings Ẑ\hat Z (the batch with its mean removed):

ℒscale=1D∑j=1D(1−σj(Ẑ))2\mathcal L_{\text{scale}}=\frac1D\sum_{j=1}^{D}\big(1-\sigma_j(\hat Z)\big)^{2}

Second a shape loss. The centred embeddings are divided by their standard deviation, Z̃=Ẑ/(sg(σ)+ϵ)\tilde Z=\hat Z/(\mathrm{sg}(\sigma)+\epsilon), where sg\mathrm{sg} is a stop-gradient, so that shape gradients do not interact with scale. They are projected on KK random unit directions wkw_k and each sorted projection is compared with the quantiles qNq_N of a standard normal, using the closed form of the one-dimensional 2-Wasserstein distance (a sliced Wasserstein distance):

ℒshape=1K∑k=1K∥sort(Z̃wk)−qN∥22\mathcal L_{\text{shape}}=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big\lVert\,\mathrm{sort}\big(\tilde Z w_k\big)-q_N\Big\rVert_2^{2}

Third a centre loss on the batch mean μ\mu. The paper's Eq. (6) writes it as the sum ‖μ‖22\lVert\mu\rVert_2^{2}, while its code listing averages over the DD coordinates; this note follows the listing, so the values below are 1D‖μ‖22\tfrac1D\lVert\mu\rVert_2^{2}. On the complete collapse the two differ by a factor of 8: 9.00019.0001 against 72.072.0. The three are combined as ℒReg=λscaleℒscale+λshapeℒshape+λcenterℒcenter\mathcal L_{\text{Reg}}=\lambda_{\text{scale}}\mathcal L_{\text{scale}}+\lambda_{\text{shape}}\mathcal L_{\text{shape}}+\lambda_{\text{center}}\mathcal L_{\text{center}}, with default weights 1 reported for high-quality datasets. The invariance term follows LeJEPA. The paper's code listing averages squared errors over both samples and slices, whereas the displayed equations write sums; this note's code follows the listing.

The paper's stated motivation has two parts. First, covariance regularisation captures only second-order statistics. Second, SIGReg's regulation signal 'diminishes as the embedding collapses'; the paper supports this with a simulated plot (its Figure 2) of gradient norm against feature scale, and states that VISReg's scale term has a gradient that approaches a constant under collapse. These are the authors' own claims and simulations. The paper's trained results, such as out-of-distribution accuracy after ImageNet pretraining, are separate evidence and are not examined here.

Prevention versus recovery, and what evidence exists for each

These two words name different questions. Prevention: if the regulariser is present from the first step, does the model ever reach a collapsed state? Recovery: if the model is already in a collapsed state, does the regulariser's gradient move it back out? A regulariser can be good at one and weak at the other, and the evidence for each is of a different kind.

  • Prevention, from training runs. VICReg's ablation and ImageNet results, LeJEPA's reported training across architectures, and VISReg's reported ImageNet pretraining are all prevention evidence from full training runs by the papers' authors. Each shows that training with the regulariser did not collapse, in the papers' settings.
  • Recovery, from toy analyses. LeJEPA optimises free embedding points directly, without any encoder, from degenerate starts, and reports that SIGReg adapts them towards an isotropic Gaussian. VISReg plots simulated gradient norms against feature scale. Both are gradient-level analyses of the regulariser, not recoveries of a trained network. Whether a trained encoder escapes a collapsed checkpoint is a separate empirical question that these analyses do not answer.

The scripts for this note run the same kind of toy analysis, with the caveat that it is a synthetic illustration. Take an isotropic Gaussian cloud, multiply it by a scale rr, and measure the gradient norm of each term with respect to the points.

Four numerical facts from Figure 5. The VICReg variance hinge written on the variance, rather than the standard deviation, has a gradient proportional to rr once r≤0.1r\le0.1 (3.1e-3 at r=0.1r=0.1, 3.1e-5 at r=10−3r=10^{-3}, 3.1e-7 at 10−510^{-5}; 2.4e-2 at r=1r=1), confirming the paper's reason for using the standard deviation. But the paper's own choice, the hinge on Var⁡+ϵ\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}+\epsilon}, is flat at about 0.011 to 0.016 while r≥ϵ=0.01r\ge\sqrt\epsilon=0.01, and falls proportionally below that (1.6e-3 at r=10−3r=10^{-3}, 1.6e-5 at 10−510^{-5}). SIGReg's gradient is proportional to rr for small rr (2.6e-2 at 10−310^{-3}, 2.6e-4 at 10−510^{-5}), which is the behaviour the VISReg paper describes. And VISReg's scale-term gradient settles at 0.031 once r≤0.1r\le0.1 (it is 1.3e-3 at r=1r=1, where the cloud already has the scale the term asks for), while the shape-term gradient grows roughly as 1/r1/r because the stop-gradient normalisation divides by the shrinking standard deviation.

A small gradient is not the same as an impossible escape. Start from the dimensional-collapse table scaled by rr, treat the points as free parameters, and run 1500 steps of plain gradient descent on the regulariser alone, with the VICReg weights μ=25,ν=1\mu=25,\nu=1, 64 fresh directions per step for SIGReg and unit weights for VISReg. The result depends on the step size and the starting scale, as the table shows.

What the table does and does not show. In this toy SIGReg restored a nearly full-rank cloud from every start, including r=10−4r=10^{-4}, where its gradient was about 10−310^{-3} of its peak: a gradient proportional to rr gives exponential growth in rr, which is slow only if the step is small. VICReg at the larger step moved the effective rank from 2 to about 5 to 6, and at the smaller step left it near 2. VISReg's three-way sum with a 1/r1/r shape gradient gave erratic results at these fixed step sizes, including a coordinate left at standard deviation 0.001. None of this says which method recovers better in training: the outcome depends on step size and loss normalisation, adaptive optimisers rescale gradients, and a real encoder constrains how points can move. It says only that a vanishing gradient norm and a failure to recover are different statements.

Geometry is not usefulness: probes and downstream checks

Every measurement above describes the shape of a cloud. None of them looks at whether the information in it is the information a task needs. The standard check is a linear probe: freeze the encoder, fit a linear classifier on the embeddings of labelled examples, and report accuracy on held-out examples. Nearest-neighbour classification, retrieval and transfer to other datasets are used in the same spirit.

The earlier Gaussian example is the cleanest statement of the problem. Two embedding tables with exactly the same distribution and the same SIGReg statistic gave probe accuracies of 98.5% and 51.0%, because one had a label tied to a coordinate and the other did not. A geometric diagnostic can show that a table has not collapsed. It cannot show that the table is useful, and a table can pass every geometric check while encoding the wrong things (for example background colour in place of object identity).

  • A healthy spectrum is necessary for most uses, not sufficient for any. Collapse destroys information; its absence does not guarantee the information is the right information.
  • Probe results have their own limits. The probe's capacity, the labelled set, the optimiser and the protocol all affect the number, so a probe accuracy is a measurement about an embedding and a protocol together.
  • Loss as a proxy. LeJEPA argues that its training loss tracks downstream quality, and the VISReg paper reports a Pearson correlation of −0.996-0.996 between its loss curve and online probe accuracy for one ViT-L/14 run on ImageNet-1K. That is an author-reported association in one setting, not a guarantee in general, and not independent confirmation.

A short checklist follows from the sections above. Centre before computing anything. Report the number of samples next to any rank. Report scale and shape separately (standard deviation or trace, and effective rank). Use relative thresholds near zero and say which precision you used. Look at the spectrum, not only its summary. Then check what the embedding is for with a downstream probe on held-out data, and treat the geometric diagnostics as early warnings rather than as a measure of quality.

Caution

Every number computed for this note comes from synthetic tables, synthetic gradient analyses and free-point optimisations made for the purpose: none is a training run or a benchmark. Where a figure is attributed to one of the papers above, such as VICReg's 57.5% or VISReg's reported correlation, it is the authors' own result, quoted and marked as theirs.
