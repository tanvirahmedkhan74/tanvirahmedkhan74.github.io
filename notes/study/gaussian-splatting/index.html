Gaussians, covariance, NeRF and 3D Gaussian splatting

What a covariance matrix really is, why its ellipse is a level set and not a surface, and how NeRF and 3D Gaussian splatting turn a scene into one pixel colour in two quite different ways.

Published 2026-11-13 · last reviewed 2026-11-13 · about 28 minutes · references

Assumes Vectors and 2x2 matrix-vector products, Eigenvectors and eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix, at the level of 'directions a matrix only stretches', Basic probability: mean, variance, a density

A Gaussian is described by a centre and a covariance matrix. The matrix is symmetric, its eigenvectors give the axes of an ellipse, and its eigenvalues give the squared half-lengths. That ellipse is a contour of a smooth density; it is not a surface and the density is not an opacity.

NeRF and 3D Gaussian splatting both produce a pixel by the same kind of front-to-back sum, but they are different answers to different questions. NeRF stores a continuous field and samples it along a camera ray. 3D Gaussian splatting stores explicit anisotropic Gaussians, projects each to a footprint on the screen, sorts them by depth and blends them.

The note keeps three ideas apart: how the scene is represented, how an image is rendered from it, and how the representation is optimised. All numerical examples are synthetic ones made for this note and are checked by a script; none of them is a result from a paper.

A Gaussian in one dimension

Suppose you measure the height of many plants and most are close to 30 cm, with fewer and fewer far from that. A Gaussian is the smooth bell-shaped curve that describes such a pile of measurements with two numbers. The mean μ\mu is where the bell is centred. The variance σ2\sigma^2 is the average squared distance from the mean; its square root σ\sigma, the standard deviation, is the typical distance from the centre and sets the width of the bell.

p(x)=12πσ2exp⁡(−(x−μ)22σ2)p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)

The curve p(x)p(x) is a density, not a probability. The probability that a value falls in a small interval is the area under the curve over that interval. A density can exceed 1: with σ2=0.04\sigma^2 = 0.04 the peak is 1/2π⋅0.04≈1.991/\sqrt{2\pi\cdot 0.04} \approx 1.99, because the bell is very narrow and the area is still 1. For the standard case σ2=1\sigma^2 = 1 the peak is 0.3990.399, and about 68.3%68.3\% of the area lies within one standard deviation of the mean.

Hold on to one detail for later. The factor in front is only a normaliser that makes the area equal 1. The shape is entirely in the exponent. Splatting renderers drop the normaliser and use just exp⁡(−12⋅distance2)\exp(-\tfrac12 \cdot\text{distance}^2), which equals 1 at the centre.

Two dimensions: a density and its ellipse

Now measure two things at once, say xx and yy positions of seeds scattered around a point. The cloud may be round, or stretched along xx, or stretched along a diagonal. A 2D Gaussian has a mean vector μ\mu and a 2×22\times 2 covariance matrix Σ\Sigma that records how wide the cloud is and in which direction it leans.

p(𝐱)=12πdet⁡Σexp⁡(−12(𝐱−μ)⊤Σ−1(𝐱−μ))p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\mu)^{\!\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu)\Big)

The quantity in the exponent, c2=(𝐱−μ)⊤Σ−1(𝐱−μ)c^2 = (\mathbf{x}-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu), is a squared distance that is measured in units of the cloud's own spread. Every point with the same value of cc has the same density, so the set of such points is a closed curve: an ellipse. Writing c=1c=1 gives the one-sigma ellipse, c=2c=2 the two-sigma ellipse. Along the ellipse of size cc the density is e−c2/2e^{-c^2/2} of its peak: 0.6070.607 for c=1c=1 and 0.1350.135 for c=2c=2. The one-sigma ellipse of a 2D Gaussian contains 1−e−1/2=39.3%1-e^{-1/2} = 39.3\% of the mass and the two-sigma ellipse 1−e−2=86.5%1-e^{-2}=86.5\%; these differ from the 68% and 95% familiar from one dimension.

Reading a 2x2 covariance matrix

Write the matrix with its entries named:

Σ=(Var⁡(x)Cov⁡(x,y)Cov⁡(y,x)Var⁡(y))=(σx2σxyσxyσy2)\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{Var}(x) & \operatorname{Cov}(x,y) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(y,x) & \operatorname{Var}(y) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_x^2 & \sigma_{xy} \\ \sigma_{xy} & \sigma_y^2 \end{pmatrix}
  • The diagonal entries are one number each: σx2\sigma_x^2 is the variance along the xx axis and σy2\sigma_y^2 the variance along the yy axis.
  • The off-diagonal entry describes how xx and yy move together. It is positive if large xx tends to come with large yy (the cloud leans up to the right), negative if large xx comes with small yy, and zero if there is no linear tendency. The correlation ρ=σxy/(σxσy)\rho = \sigma_{xy}/(\sigma_x\sigma_y) rescales it to lie between −1-1 and 11.

The off-diagonal number is one number written in two places. By definition Cov⁡(x,y)\operatorname{Cov}(x,y) is the average of (x−μx)(y−μy)(x-\mu_x)(y-\mu_y), and multiplication does not care about order, so Cov⁡(x,y)=Cov⁡(y,x)\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)=\operatorname{Cov}(y,x). That is what it means for a covariance matrix to be symmetric: Σ=Σ⊤\Sigma = \Sigma^{\top}. There is no separate top-right and bottom-left value to tune.

Worked example

Start from the valid matrix Σ0=(20.60.61)\Sigma_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. You want the cloud to lean more steeply, so you change the correlation entry to 1.21.2. A common slip is to edit only the top-right entry:

M=(21.20.61)M≠M⊤M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1.2 \\ 0.6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\qquad M \neq M^{\top}
  1. MM claims Cov⁡(x,y)=1.2\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)=1.2 in its top row and Cov⁡(y,x)=0.6\operatorname{Cov}(y,x)=0.6 in its bottom row. Those are the same quantity, so no cloud of points can produce MM. It is not a covariance matrix, however innocent it looks.
  2. The tools of the next section also break. A symmetric matrix has perpendicular eigenvectors. MM has eigenvalues 2.4852.485 and 0.5150.515 with eigenvectors (0.927,0.375)(0.927, 0.375) and (−0.629,0.778)(-0.629, 0.778), whose dot product is −0.29-0.29 rather than 00. There is no pair of perpendicular axes and so no ellipse to draw.
  3. The correct edit changes both entries together: Σ1=(21.21.21)\Sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. This one is symmetric, with eigenvalues 2.82.8 and 0.20.2, correlation ρ=1.2/2⋅1=0.849\rho = 1.2/\sqrt{2\cdot 1} = 0.849, and an ellipse leaning at 33.7∘33.7^{\circ}.

The structural remedy is to never store the off-diagonal entry twice. Store the three free numbers σx2,σxy,σy2\sigma_x^2,\ \sigma_{xy},\ \sigma_y^2, or build Σ\Sigma from a rotation and scales as in the splatting section below, where the product RSS⊤R⊤R S S^{\top} R^{\top} is symmetric by construction.

A symmetric 2×22\times2 matrix has exactly three free numbers; a symmetric 3×33\times3 matrix, which 3D Gaussians use, has six.

Eigenvectors and eigenvalues give the ellipse

Because Σ\Sigma is symmetric it can always be written Σ=VΛV⊤\Sigma = V\Lambda V^{\top}, where the columns of VV are perpendicular unit eigenvectors and Λ=diag⁡(λ1,λ2)\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2) holds the eigenvalues. Read this as three steps: rotate so the eigenvectors lie along the coordinate axes, stretch each axis by its eigenvalue, rotate back. The cloud is therefore a plain axis-aligned cloud viewed in a rotated frame.

  • The eigenvectors are the directions of the ellipse axes.
  • The eigenvalue λi\lambda_i is the variance along its axis, so the cc-sigma ellipse has semi-axis length cλic\sqrt{\lambda_i} along eigenvector viv_i.
  • The ellipse is the image of the unit circle: {μ+cVΛ1/2𝐮:∥𝐮∥=1}\{\mu + c\,V\Lambda^{1/2}\mathbf{u} : \|\mathbf{u}\|=1\}. Substituting this into the exponent gives exactly c2c^2, which is why it is the level set at height e−c2/2e^{-c^2/2}.

Worked example

Take Σ1=(21.21.21)\Sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&1.2\\1.2&1\end{pmatrix}. The trace is 33 and the determinant is 2⋅1−1.22=0.562\cdot1-1.2^2 = 0.56. The characteristic equation λ2−3λ+0.56=0\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 0.56 = 0 gives

λ=3±9−2.242=3±2.62∈{2.8,0.2}\lambda = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{9-2.24}}{2} = \frac{3\pm 2.6}{2} \in \{2.8,\ 0.2\}

The major eigenvector is (0.832,0.555)(0.832, 0.555), angle 33.7∘33.7^{\circ} from the xx axis; the minor one is (−0.555,0.832)(-0.555, 0.832). The one-sigma ellipse has half-lengths 2.8=1.673\sqrt{2.8}=1.673 and 0.2=0.447\sqrt{0.2}=0.447. Going the other way, the point μ+2.8(0.832,0.555)\mu + \sqrt{2.8}\,(0.832,0.555) satisfies (𝐱−μ)⊤Σ−1(𝐱−μ)=1(\mathbf{x}-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu) = 1 to machine precision.

Which matrices are allowed

A symmetric matrix is a valid covariance matrix only if it is positive semidefinite (PSD): every eigenvalue is at least zero. Equivalently, the variance along every direction 𝐮\mathbf{u}, which is 𝐮⊤Σ𝐮\mathbf{u}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{u}, is non-negative, as any variance must be. It is positive definite (PD) when every eigenvalue is strictly greater than zero. For a symmetric 2×22\times 2 matrix with entries a,b,da, b, d the quick test is:

Σis PSD⇔a≥0,d≥0,ad−b2≥0(PD: all strict)\Sigma\ \text{is PSD} \iff a \ge 0,\ d \ge 0,\ ad-b^2 \ge 0\qquad(\text{PD: all strict})

PD matters in practice. The density formula uses Σ−1\Sigma^{-1} and det⁡Σ\sqrt{\det\Sigma}, so it needs det⁡Σ>0\det\Sigma > 0. A PSD matrix with a zero eigenvalue, such as (1111)\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} (eigenvalues 00 and 22), is a Gaussian squashed onto a line: legitimate as a limit but with no ordinary density.

Caution

The last matrix is symmetric and looks plausible, but σxy=2\sigma_{xy}=2 is larger than σxσy=1\sigma_x\sigma_y = 1, so the implied correlation would be 22. Its determinant is 1−4=−31-4=-3 and one eigenvalue is −1-1. That would be a variance of −1-1 along the direction (1,−1)/2(1,-1)/\sqrt2, which cannot exist. Plugging it into the density formula would produce an exponent that grows without bound along that direction, not a bell. The figure therefore shows a crossed-out panel: there is no ellipse to draw, so nothing is rendered.

Symmetry is necessary but not sufficient. For a 2×22\times2 matrix, the requirement |σxy|≤σxσy|\sigma_{xy}|\le\sigma_x\sigma_y (correlation between −1-1 and 11) is exactly the condition ad−b2≥0ad-b^2\ge 0.

This is the reason the 3D Gaussian splatting paper does not optimise the covariance entries directly. Gradient steps can easily produce an invalid matrix, so the paper stores a scale vector and a rotation and builds Σ=RSS⊤R⊤\Sigma = R S S^{\top} R^{\top}, which is positive semidefinite whatever values the optimiser reaches (Kerbl et al., Sec. 4).

The ellipse is a level set, not a surface

Three statements are easy to blur together, and splatting depends on keeping them apart.

  • A Gaussian density is a smooth function on all of space. It is positive everywhere, however small far away. It has no edge.
  • An ellipse (in 3D, an ellipsoid) is one contour of that function: the set where the exponent equals c2c^2. Choosing cc is a choice about what to draw. The same Gaussian has one-sigma, two-sigma and three-sigma ellipses, nested and all equally real.
  • A Gaussian is therefore not a mesh ellipsoid with a solid skin, and the density itself is not an opacity. Opacity is a separate number a renderer attaches. In splatting the footprint on the screen is the Gaussian value multiplied by a per-splat opacity, and the result is a soft blob that fades out smoothly.

Note also the units. The normalised density of a 2D Gaussian has peak 1/(2πdet⁡Σ)1/(2\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma}), which is 0.2130.213 for Σ1\Sigma_1 and can be far above 1 for a tiny covariance. The unnormalised exp⁡(−c2/2)\exp(-c^2/2) that splatting uses peaks at 1 and is only a profile. Neither is, by itself, an opacity.

From a pixel to a camera ray

A pinhole camera sits at a point 𝐨\mathbf{o} and sees the world through an image plane. Every pixel corresponds to exactly one line from 𝐨\mathbf{o} into the scene, the ray through that pixel. Rendering a pixel means asking what light arrives along its ray.

In camera coordinates (with zz pointing forward) a 3D point (X,Y,Z)(X,Y,Z) lands on pixel (u,v)(u,v) by perspective division and the intrinsic matrix KK with focal length ff in pixels and principal point (cx,cy)(c_x,c_y):

u=fXZ+cx,v=fYZ+cyu = f\,\frac{X}{Z} + c_x,\qquad v = f\,\frac{Y}{Z} + c_y

Run it backwards. A pixel fixes only the ratios X/ZX/Z and Y/ZY/Z, so it determines a direction, not a point: 𝐝cam=K−1(u,v,1)⊤=((u−cx)/f,(v−cy)/f,1)\mathbf{d}_{\text{cam}} = K^{-1}(u,v,1)^{\top} = \big((u-c_x)/f,\ (v-c_y)/f,\ 1\big). Rotate that direction into world coordinates with the camera-to-world rotation and the ray is

𝐫(t)=𝐨+t𝐝,t∈[tn,tf]\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\,\mathbf{d},\qquad t \in [t_n, t_f]

Worked example

A synthetic camera with f=800f=800 and principal point (480,300)(480,300) looks at pixel (640,300)(640,300). Then 𝐝cam=(160/800,0,1)=(0.2,0,1)\mathbf{d}_{\text{cam}} = (160/800,\ 0,\ 1) = (0.2, 0, 1), normalised (0.196,0,0.981)(0.196, 0, 0.981). As a check, the point (0.4,0,2)(0.4,0,2) lies on this ray (it is 2𝐝cam2\mathbf{d}_{\text{cam}}) and projects to u=800⋅0.4/2+480=640u=800\cdot0.4/2+480 = 640, v=300v=300, the same pixel.

NeRF: sampling a continuous field along the ray

In Mildenhall et al. the scene is a function, not a list of objects. A neural network maps a 3D position and a viewing direction to a colour 𝐜\mathbf{c} and a volume density σ\sigma. The density is read as the differential probability that a ray is stopped by an infinitesimal particle at that point. Nothing in this representation is a point, a Gaussian or a mesh: the field exists at every location the network can be queried.

The expected colour of a camera ray 𝐫(t)=𝐨+t𝐝\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{o}+t\mathbf{d} is, in the paper's Eq. 1,

C(𝐫)=∫tntfT(t)σ(𝐫(t))𝐜(𝐫(t),𝐝)dt,T(t)=exp⁡(−∫tntσ(𝐫(s))ds)C(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf{r}(t))\,\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{r}(t),\mathbf{d})\,dt,\qquad T(t)=\exp\!\Big(-\int_{t_n}^{t}\sigma(\mathbf{r}(s))\,ds\Big)

T(t)T(t), the transmittance, is the probability that the ray reaches distance tt without being stopped. It starts at 1 and only decreases, and dense regions make it fall quickly. The integrand says: colour contributes in proportion to the chance of getting there (TT) times the chance of stopping there (σ\sigma) times what colour is emitted (𝐜\mathbf{c}).

The integral cannot be done in closed form for a neural field, so NeRF estimates it. The paper splits [tn,tf][t_n,t_f] into NN equal bins, draws one random sample per bin (stratified sampling), and uses the quadrature rule from Max's volume rendering review. With δi=ti+1−ti\delta_i = t_{i+1}-t_i (paper Eq. 3):

Ĉ(𝐫)=∑i=1NTiαi𝐜i,αi=1−exp⁡(−σiδi),Ti=exp⁡(−∑j=1i−1σjδj)=∏j=1i−1(1−αj)\hat C(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\,\alpha_i\,\mathbf{c}_i,\qquad \alpha_i = 1-\exp(-\sigma_i\delta_i),\qquad T_i=\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\sigma_j\delta_j\Big)=\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-\alpha_j)

The paper itself remarks that this reduces to ordinary alpha compositing with αi=1−exp⁡(−σiδi)\alpha_i = 1-\exp(-\sigma_i\delta_i). Notice how αi\alpha_i depends on both the density and the step length: σ\sigma is a rate per unit length, and α\alpha is the fraction of light absorbed in a step.

For a clean check the sample sits at each bin centre instead of a random point, the density is a made-up curve with two bumps (near t=3.5t=3.5 and t=4.8t=4.8), and the colour is red before t=4.2t=4.2 and blue after. Two things NeRF does not do. It does not project explicit point Gaussians onto the image, and it does not sort primitives. It asks the field a question at many places along each ray and sums the answers. Rendering one image therefore needs a network evaluation for every sample on every pixel's ray, which is why the original method is slow to render.

3D Gaussian splatting: an explicit scene of Gaussians

Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler and Drettakis (project page: 3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering) represent the scene as a long list of anisotropic 3D Gaussians (anisotropic means stretched by different amounts in different directions). Each one has a position μ\mu, a 3×33\times3 covariance Σ\Sigma, an opacity oo, and view-dependent colour stored as spherical-harmonic coefficients (a compact way to store a colour that changes with the viewing angle). The scene is the list itself, which can be inspected, edited and counted.

The paper defines each Gaussian without a normalising constant (Eq. 4) and builds its covariance from a rotation and scale so that it is always valid (Eq. 6):

G(𝐱)=exp⁡(−12(𝐱−μ)⊤Σ−1(𝐱−μ)),Σ=RSS⊤R⊤G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\mu)^{\!\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mu)\Big),\qquad \Sigma = R\,S\,S^{\top}R^{\top}

Here SS is a diagonal matrix of three scales and RR a rotation, which the paper stores as a scale vector and a quaternion. This is the eigendecomposition of the previous sections in three dimensions: the columns of RR are the ellipsoid axes and the diagonal of SS⊤S S^{\top} holds the eigenvalues. RSS⊤R⊤R S S^{\top} R^{\top} is symmetric and positive semidefinite for any RR and SS, so the optimiser can never produce the invalid matrices of the earlier section. The ellipsoid is again a level set of the density, not a surface.

Projecting a 3D Gaussian to a screen footprint

To draw a 3D Gaussian we need its footprint on the image: a 2D Gaussian with its own mean and 2×22\times2 covariance Σ′\Sigma'. The mean simply projects like any point. The covariance is harder because perspective projection is not linear, and a linear map is exactly what keeps a Gaussian a Gaussian.

The lineage here is the elliptical weighted average (EWA) splatting work of Zwicker, Pfister, van Baar and Gross. Their paper maps each Gaussian kernel into ray space with the local affine approximation of the projective transformation: the first two terms of its Taylor expansion at the Gaussian's centre 𝐭k\mathbf{t}_k, φk(𝐭)=𝐱k+Jk(𝐭−𝐭k)\varphi_k(\mathbf{t}) = \mathbf{x}_k + J_k(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{t}_k), where JkJ_k is the Jacobian (matrix of partial derivatives) at that point. They state that this mapping is exact only for the ray through the centre, that it replaces perspective projection locally by an oblique orthographic one, and that errors grow with ray divergence. 3D Gaussian splatting uses it for the covariance (paper Eq. 5), given a viewing transformation WW:

Σ′=JWΣW⊤J⊤\Sigma' = J\,W\,\Sigma\,W^{\top}J^{\top}

WW rotates the world covariance into camera axes. JJ is the Jacobian of the perspective map at the Gaussian's camera-space centre (tx,ty,tz)(t_x,t_y,t_z). After the product the paper skips the third row and column, leaving the 2×22\times2 matrix. With focal lengths fx,fyf_x, f_y the surviving two rows of JJ are:

J=(fx/tz0−fxtx/tz20fy/tz−fyty/tz2)J = \begin{pmatrix} f_x/t_z & 0 & -f_x t_x/t_z^2 \\ 0 & f_y/t_z & -f_y t_y/t_z^2 \end{pmatrix}

The structure of these rows matches the Jacobian in the EWA paper (its Eq. 29), up to the focal-length scaling to pixels and dropping the depth row. Because the formula has the shape AΣA⊤A\Sigma A^{\top}, symmetry and positive semidefiniteness carry through to Σ′\Sigma' automatically.

Caution

This is an approximation, not the exact projection. The true image of a 3D Gaussian under perspective division is not a Gaussian at all. The Jacobian replaces the projection by its tangent at the centre, which is accurate when the Gaussian is small compared with its distance from the camera and degrades for large or very close Gaussians and for splats far from the image centre.

To see the size of the approximation, draw 400,000 seeded samples of the 3D Gaussian, project each one exactly, and take the sample covariance: (1355528528713)\begin{pmatrix}1355&528\\528&713\end{pmatrix}, within about 2% of Σ′\Sigma'. Depth sorting and front-to-back alpha compositing

Each projected Gaussian contributes, at pixel 𝐩\mathbf{p}, an alpha value equal to its opacity times its 2D Gaussian profile evaluated at that pixel (the paper describes this as a 2D Gaussian with covariance multiplied by a learned per-point opacity):

αi=oiexp⁡(−12(𝐩−μi′)⊤Σi′−1(𝐩−μi′))\alpha_i = o_i\,\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{p}-\mu_i')^{\!\top}\Sigma_i'^{-1}(\mathbf{p}-\mu_i')\Big)

The renderer sorts the splats by depth (the paper does this on the GPU with a radix sort, in tiles of the image) and blends them from nearest to farthest. This is the paper's Eq. 3:

C=∑i𝐜iαi∏j=1i−1(1−αj)C = \sum_{i} \mathbf{c}_i\,\alpha_i\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-\alpha_j)

The product is the transmittance TiT_i again: how much light is left after the nearer splats. This is the same image-formation equation as NeRF's Eq. 3; what differs is where the αi\alpha_i and 𝐜i\mathbf{c}_i come from. In NeRF they come from querying a field at samples along a ray. In splatting they come from evaluating a projected footprint for each sorted primitive. The paper notes its blending can be approximate in some configurations, because it sorts per tile, not per pixel.

Representation, rendering and optimisation

Methods in this area are often described as a single thing, which hides what actually changed. It helps to ask three separate questions of any of them.

  • Representation. What object holds the scene? A neural field in NeRF; a list of anisotropic Gaussians with opacity and colour in 3DGS. Everything in the sections on covariance, ellipses and positive semidefiniteness belongs here.
  • Rendering. How does a camera turn that object into pixels? Ray sampling and volume compositing for NeRF; projection, depth sorting and alpha compositing for 3DGS. Rays, the Jacobian and the TiαiT_i\alpha_i sum belong here. Rendering is a fixed procedure that is differentiable but holds no learned knowledge by itself.
  • Optimisation. How are the stored numbers fitted to photographs? Both methods compare rendered and real images and follow gradients through the renderer. 3DGS additionally adds, splits and removes Gaussians during training, and its choice to store scale and rotation rather than raw covariance entries is an optimisation decision about keeping Σ\Sigma valid.

Seen this way, NeRF and 3DGS share a compositing formula and a training signal, and differ mainly in representation and in the rendering algorithm that representation makes cheap. This note has described the first two. How the Gaussians are initialised, densified and pruned is the optimisation side and is outside its scope.
