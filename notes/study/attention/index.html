Attention, from three tokens to million-token models

One small worked example, then the efficient variants sorted by what they actually change: the mixing rule, the pairs, the execution, or the cache.

Published 2026-11-13 · last reviewed 2026-11-13 · about 30 minutes · references

Assumes Matrix multiplication and dot products, Softmax as a way to turn scores into weights

Attention lets every token in a sequence read from every other token. For each token it scores how well that token's question matches every other token's label, turns the scores into weights that sum to one, and returns a weighted mix of what those tokens carry. The note computes this by hand on three tokens, with every matrix written out and every shape stated.

The expensive part is the score matrix, which has one entry per pair of tokens and so grows with the square of the sequence length. Methods that make attention cheaper fall into four different kinds, and mixing them up is the most common source of confusion: some change the mixing rule, some change which pairs are computed, some change only how the same arithmetic is executed on hardware, and some change what is stored for decoding.

The last section is dated 13 November 2026. It separates what Moonshot's technical report documents for Kimi K3 from what is not documented, and teaches the mechanisms by the papers that define them.

The idea in one sentence

Take the sentence "the cat sat". When a model processes the word "sat", it helps to look back at "cat" to know who is sitting. Attention is the operation that does this looking back in a learned, differentiable way. Each token produces a query (what it is looking for), a key (how it can be found) and a value (what it hands over when found). A token's output is a mix of all values, weighted by how well its query matches each key.

In the notation of Vaswani et al. (arXiv:1706.03762), with Q,K∈ℝn×dkQ, K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_k} and V∈ℝn×dvV \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_v} for a sequence of nn tokens, the whole operation is

Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax(QK⊤dk)V\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V

The rest of this note is just this line, unpacked with numbers, then asked a practical question: what does it cost, and what can be changed without changing the answer.

What rows and columns mean. In SS and AA, the row index ii is the query token (the one doing the reading) and the column index jj is the key token (the one being read). Each row of AA sums to 1 (to rounding: 1.001,1.000,1.0001.001, 1.000, 1.000 at three decimals; in exact arithmetic every row is exactly 1). Columns do not sum to 1. In Q,K,V,OQ, K, V, O a row is one token and a column is one learned feature of that token.

Check it yourself

Query 1 has equal scores for keys 1 and 3 (both 2.8282.828) and a much lower score for key 2 (0). Before looking at AA, predict which two entries of row 1 are equal and which is smallest. Then confirm that row 3 puts most weight on key 3 because its score 4.2434.243 is the largest, and that the weights are not proportional to the scores: softmax exponentiates, so a gap of about 0.70.7 in score turns into a factor of about 22 in weight.

Self-attention, cross-attention, masks and heads

Self-attention versus cross-attention. In self-attention, QQ, KK and VV are all projections of the same sequence XX, as above. In cross-attention, the queries come from one sequence and the keys and values from another. In the encoder–decoder Transformer of Vaswani et al., decoder states supply QQ (shape ndec×dkn_{\text{dec}}\times d_k) and encoder outputs supply KK and VV (shape m×dkm\times d_k and m×dvm\times d_v), so the score matrix is rectangular, ndec×mn_{\text{dec}}\times m. Rows still index the reader and columns still index what is read.

Causal masking. A language model that predicts the next token must not read tokens that come later. The fix is to set the score of every pair with j>ij>i to −∞-\infty before the softmax, so its weight is exactly 00. For the example above:

Smasked=[2.828−∞−∞2.8281.414−∞3.5360.7074.243]⇒Acausal≈[1.000000.8040.19600.3240.0190.657]S_{\text{masked}}=\begin{bmatrix}2.828&-\infty&-\infty\\2.828&1.414&-\infty\\3.536&0.707&4.243\end{bmatrix}\;\Rightarrow\;A_{\text{causal}}\approx\begin{bmatrix}1.000&0&0\\0.804&0.196&0\\0.324&0.019&0.657\end{bmatrix}

Row 1 can only see itself, so its weight is 11 and its output is V1=(2,1)V_1=(2,1). Row 2 renormalises over two keys: e2.828=16.919e^{2.828}=16.919 and e1.414=4.113e^{1.414}=4.113 sum to 21.03221.032, giving (0.804,0.196)(0.804, 0.196) and output (1.609,1.391)(1.609, 1.391). Multiple heads. One attention head can express one pattern of "who reads whom" at a time. Multi-head attention runs hh heads in parallel. What is split: the projections. Each head has its own WQ(i),WK(i),WV(i)W_Q^{(i)}, W_K^{(i)}, W_V^{(i)} mapping dmodeld_{\text{model}} to dkd_k and dvd_v; in the original Transformer, dmodel=512d_{\text{model}}=512, h=8h=8, dk=dv=64d_k=d_v=64. What is concatenated: the head outputs, each n×dvn\times d_v, are joined side by side into an n×hdvn\times h d_v matrix and multiplied by an output projection WO∈ℝhdv×dmodelW_O\in\mathbb{R}^{hd_v\times d_{\text{model}}}. The worked example is a single head; with dmodel=4d_{\text{model}}=4 and h=2h=2, a second head with its own 4×24\times2 matrices would give a second 3×23\times2 output, the two would be concatenated to 3×43\times4, and WOW_O would be 4×44\times4.

Why position must be added

Look again at the worked example: if two tokens swapped places in XX, the rows of QQ, KK and VV would swap with them and the output rows would swap too. Attention treats its input as a set. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would be indistinguishable. Order has to be injected, and the main families differ in where they inject it.

Four families of positional information. The last two act inside the score computation, not on the input embedding.
FamilyWhere position entersOne-line distinction
Absolute, fixedAdded to the token embedding before the first layerSinusoids of different frequencies, as in Vaswani et al. (arXiv:1706.03762); no parameters to learn.
Absolute, learnedAdded to the embedding, from a trained table indexed by positionA lookup table with one vector per position; has no entry beyond the longest training length.
RoPEQueries and keys are rotated by an angle that depends on positionThe dot product then depends on the relative offset i−ji-j; Su et al. (arXiv:2104.09864).
ALiBiA penalty proportional to distance is subtracted from the scoresNo position embedding at all; Press et al. (arXiv:2108.12409) report extrapolation to longer inputs than seen in training.

Recurrent and state-space layers carry order in their update rule, so a hybrid model can rely on them for position. The dated section below returns to this: Moonshot's Kimi K3 report describes global-attention layers that use no explicit positional encoding.

What attention costs

Every statement about cost needs four things attached: the sequence length nn, the per-head width dd (written dhd_h for the per-head dimension of softmax attention, and dk,dvd_k, d_v for state dimensions), the number of heads hh, and whether the setting is training or prefill — prefill being the pass that processes a prompt before any token is generated, so all nn tokens are handled at once or single-token decode (one new token attends to nn cached tokens). Comparisons that omit these are not comparisons.

Training, one layer, one sequence. The score matrix QK⊤QK^{\top} has n×nn\times n entries per head, so it is O(n2)O(n^2) in memory if stored. Computing it takes about 2n2dh2n^2d_h FLOPs (one multiply and one add per term) and applying AA to VV takes another 2n2dh2n^2d_h, for about 4n2dh4n^2d_h FLOPs per head, ignoring the softmax's O(n2)O(n^2) exponentials. Doubling nn quadruples both. Illustrative arithmetic with stated dimensions: n=4096n=4096, dh=64d_h=64, h=32h=32, 16-bit storage. Per head, n2=16,777,216n^2 = 16{,}777{,}216 scores, which is 33.6 MB; across 32 heads, about 1.07 GB per layer per sequence if the scores are materialised. The FLOP count is 4n2dh=4.29×1094n^2d_h=4.29\times10^9 per head and 1.37×10111.37\times10^{11} across 32 heads. Raising nn from 4096 to 32,768 multiplies these by 6464. These are counts from formulas, not measured timings.

Single-token decode, one layer. The new token's query is scored against nn cached keys, then mixes nn cached values: about 4ndh4nd_h FLOPs per head, which is linear in nn, plus reading the whole cache. For n=4096n=4096, dh=64d_h=64 that is 1.05×1061.05\times10^6 FLOPs per head. The dominant cost is memory traffic, because the cache holds 2hkvdhn2\,h_{kv}\,d_h\,n numbers per layer: for hkv=h=32h_{kv}=h=32 in 16-bit that is 33.6 MB per layer per sequence, and every generated token must read it again.

Four different things "efficient attention" can mean

Methods marketed as efficient attention change one of four distinct things. Keeping them apart answers most of the questions people ask about them, for example whether a method is exact, whether it shrinks compute or only memory, and whether it helps training or decoding.

A bucket describes the change, not the model. A real system combines several buckets.

(a) Changing the mixing rule

Linear (kernel) attention. Softmax couples the query with all keys in a way that forces the n×nn\times n matrix to be formed. Katharopoulos et al. (arXiv:2006.16236, ICML 2020) replace exp⁡(q⊤k)\exp(q^{\top}k) by a product of feature maps, ϕ(q)⊤ϕ(k)\phi(q)^{\top}\phi(k), with ϕ(x)=elu(x)+1\phi(x)=\mathrm{elu}(x)+1. Matrix multiplication is associative, so the order of the product can be changed:

V′i=ϕ(Qi)⊤∑jϕ(Kj)Vj⊤ϕ(Qi)⊤∑jϕ(Kj)V'_i=\frac{\phi(Q_i)^{\top}\sum_{j}\phi(K_j)V_j^{\top}}{\phi(Q_i)^{\top}\sum_{j}\phi(K_j)}

The sum ∑jϕ(Kj)Vj⊤\sum_j\phi(K_j)V_j^{\top} is a dk×dvd_k\times d_v matrix that does not depend on ii, so it is computed once. In the causal case it becomes a running state Si=Si−1+ϕ(Ki)Vi⊤S_i=S_{i-1}+\phi(K_i)V_i^{\top} with normaliser Zi=Zi−1+ϕ(Ki)Z_i=Z_{i-1}+\phi(K_i), and the output is ϕ(Qi)⊤Si/ϕ(Qi)⊤Zi\phi(Q_i)^{\top}S_i/\phi(Q_i)^{\top}Z_i. This is a recurrent network, which is the title's point. Training cost is O(ndkdv)O(n\,d_k d_v), linear in nn; decoding carries only SS and ZZ and does O(dkdv)O(d_kd_v) work per token.

This is a different function, not a faster way to compute the same one. Running it on the worked example's QQ and KK (a toy check made for this note) gives causal outputs (2,1)(2,1), (1.2,1.8)(1.2,1.8), (1.6,1.829)(1.6,1.829), whereas causal softmax attention gave (2,1)(2,1), (1.609,1.391)(1.609,1.391), (1.962,1.695)(1.962,1.695). Row 1 agrees only because both see a single key. The quadratic form and the recurrent form of linear attention agree exactly with each other, which the verification script checks.

Recurrent and state-space layers. A state-space model keeps a state hth_t updated by ht=A‾ht−1+B‾xth_t=\bar A h_{t-1}+\bar B x_t and reads it with yt=Chty_t=Ch_t. Gu and Dao (arXiv:2312.00752) make the parameters depend on the input ("selective"), so the model can choose per token what to keep or forget, with a hardware-aware scan; the authors report linear scaling in sequence length. Linear attention and these models are close relatives: both compress the past into a fixed-size state.

The delta rule. A plain running sum Si=Si−1+kivi⊤S_i=S_{i-1}+k_iv_i^{\top} only adds. Delta-rule layers first erase what the state currently stores under key ktk_t and then write the new value. The Kimi K3 technical report writes its Kimi Delta Attention update as

St=(I−βtktkt⊤Diag(αt))St−1+βtktvt⊤,õt=St⊤qtS_t=\left(I-\beta_t\,k_tk_t^{\top}\,\mathrm{Diag}(\alpha_t)\right)S_{t-1}+\beta_t\,k_tv_t^{\top},\qquad \tilde o_t=S_t^{\top}q_t

with St∈ℝdk×dvS_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_k\times d_v}, a per-channel retention factor αt∈(0,1)dk\alpha_t\in(0,1)^{d_k} that lets each key channel forget at its own rate, and a write strength βt∈(0,1)\beta_t\in(0,1) (K3 report, Eq. 1, which states that it follows the Kimi Linear report, arXiv:2510.26692).

Caution

The limitation is structural. A state of dk×dvd_k\times d_v numbers is a fixed-size summary. Storing nn arbitrary key–value pairs for exact lookup needs on the order of ndvn\,d_v numbers, so once nn exceeds roughly dkd_k a fixed state must compress, and some exact recall is lost. This is why associative recall, retrieving a specific earlier item, is the usual stress test for such models, and why current large systems keep some full-attention layers (see the dated section). That argument is general background, not a measured result from this note. The Kimi Linear abstract itself frames the goal as making better use of limited finite-state memory.

(b) Changing which pairs are computed

Here the formula stays softmax attention, but each query is only allowed to look at a subset of keys. Everything outside the subset is treated as masked, exactly like the causal mask, so the missing entries are never computed.

  • Sliding window. Token ii attends to the previous ww tokens. Pairs kept: about nwnw instead of n2/2n^2/2, so compute is O(nwd)O(nwd) and the score memory is O(nw)O(nw). Information from farther away can still travel, but only by hopping through layers.
  • Hybrid local and global. Longformer (Beltagy et al., arXiv:2004.05150) combines windowed attention with task-chosen global tokens that attend to, and are attended by, everyone; the authors state that its attention scales linearly in sequence length.
  • Structured sparse. Sparse Transformers (Child et al., arXiv:1904.10509) factorise the attention matrix into patterns such as a local band plus a strided set and reduce the cost to roughly O(nn)O(n\sqrt{n}).

Bucket (b) is the only one here that is an approximation by omission: if the true attention weight on a dropped pair was large, that signal is lost. That trade is acceptable when the task is mostly local, and it is why these patterns are often paired with a few global positions or with occasional full-attention layers.

(c) Changing execution only: FlashAttention

Definition

FlashAttention is exact attention. It is not linear attention, it is not sparse and it is not an approximation. It computes the same softmax(QK⊤/dk)V\mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})V as the code that builds the full matrix, and gives the same result up to floating-point rounding. It does not change the mixing rule.

What it changes is where the intermediate numbers live. On a GPU the large main memory is slow compared with the small on-chip memory. A naive implementation writes the n×nn\times n score matrix to main memory, reads it back for the softmax, writes the weights, and reads them again for the multiplication by VV, so memory traffic, not arithmetic, is the bottleneck. Dao et al. (arXiv:2205.14135) split QQ, KK, VV into tiles that fit on chip, process one tile pair at a time with an online softmax that keeps a running maximum and running sum so the normalisation can be completed without ever holding a full row, and never write the n×nn\times n matrix out. The paper's Theorem 2 states that standard attention needs Θ(Nd+N2)\Theta(Nd+N^2) main-memory accesses while FlashAttention needs Θ(N2d2M−1)\Theta(N^2d^2M^{-1}) for on-chip size MM, which is many times fewer for typical dd and MM; the forward pass needs only O(N)O(N) extra memory; and a further proposition shows that no exact attention algorithm can asymptotically improve on that number of accesses over a range of MM.

In the cost vocabulary of this note: the FLOP count is still O(n2d)O(n^2d), the work is still quadratic in nn, and the stored extra memory drops from O(n2)O(n^2) to O(n)O(n). FlashAttention-2 (Dao, arXiv:2307.08691) keeps the same exactness and reworks how the work is divided among the GPU’s thread blocks and warps (a warp being the group of threads a GPU runs in lockstep). Because it is exact, an exact kernel can be combined with any model that uses softmax attention; it also combines with bucket (b), since the FlashAttention paper extends the same tiling to block-sparse patterns. One side effect worth knowing about: because the order of floating-point operations differs from a naive implementation, exact here means mathematically identical, not bit-for-bit identical.

(d) Changing the KV cache and head sharing

During generation, each past token's keys and values are stored so they need not be recomputed. For standard multi-head attention (MHA) that is 2hdh2\,h\,d_h numbers per token per layer. At long contexts, reading this cache for every new token is what limits decoding speed. Three methods shrink it without changing the softmax rule itself.

  • Multi-query attention (MQA). Shazeer (arXiv:1911.02150) lets all query heads share a single key head and a single value head. Cache per token per layer: 2dh2d_h.
  • Grouped-query attention (GQA). Ainslie et al. (arXiv:2305.13245, EMNLP 2023) use gg key–value heads, with 1<g<h1<g<h and each shared by h/gh/g query heads. Cache: 2gdh2\,g\,d_h. g=1g=1 is MQA and g=hg=h is MHA. The paper also shows how to convert an existing multi-head checkpoint with a small amount of additional training, which it states as about 5% of the original pre-training compute.
  • Multi-head latent attention (MLA). DeepSeek-V2 (arXiv:2405.04434) compresses keys and values jointly into a low-dimensional latent, ctKV=WDKVht∈ℝdcc_t^{KV}=W^{DKV}h_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_c}, and recovers per-head keys and values with up-projections, ktC=WUKctKVk^C_t=W^{UK}c^{KV}_t and vtC=WUVctKVv^C_t=W^{UV}c^{KV}_t. Only ctKVc_t^{KV} is cached. Because rotary embedding does not commute with those up-projections, V2 adds a small decoupled rotary key ktRk_t^R that is cached as well, for a cache of (dc+dhR)(d_c+d_h^R) numbers per token per layer. DeepSeek-V3 (arXiv:2412.19437) also uses MLA.

A worked comparison with stated dimensions, for one layer, one sequence, decode with cache length n=4096n=4096, h=32h=32 heads, dh=64d_h=64, 16-bit storage. MHA: 2⋅32⋅64⋅4096⋅22\cdot32\cdot64\cdot4096\cdot2 bytes =33.6=33.6 MB. GQA with g=8g=8: 8.48.4 MB, a factor of 4 smaller. MQA: 1.051.05 MB, a factor of 32. MLA with the V2 setting dc=4dhd_c=4d_h, dhR=dh/2d_h^R=d_h/2: 288288 numbers per token, 2.362.36 MB, a factor of about 1414 below MHA. The DeepSeek-V2 paper states that this equals GQA with 2.25 groups. These factors come from the formulas, with the layout stated; real systems differ in precision, layout and number of cached layers.

All three leave the arithmetic of softmax(QK⊤)V\mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top})V per head intact; they change how many distinct K,VK,V streams there are. They therefore mainly help decode bandwidth and the number of sequences that fit in memory, not the quadratic training compute.

Current developments (checked 13 November 2026)

This section is a dated snapshot. Model descriptions below were checked against primary documents on 13 November 2026; they can change, and a name alone is never evidence of a mechanism.

Kimi K3. Moonshot AI's site links to the Kimi K3 announcement (kimi.ai/blog/kimi-k3), and the official repository MoonshotAI/Kimi-K3 carries a model card and the full technical report, Kimi K3: Open Frontier Intelligence (Kimi Team; PDF in the repository). That report documents the attention design, so it can be described from the source rather than inferred. It is a company technical report, not a peer-reviewed paper.

  • Layout. Each block has 3 Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) layers followed by 1 Gated MLA layer, a 3:1 ratio, with one extra Gated MLA layer at the end of the backbone so the final layer is global. The model card lists 93 layers: 69 KDA plus 24 Gated MLA, 96 attention heads, attention hidden dimension 7168, and a context length of 1,048,576 tokens.
  • KDA layers. A delta-rule recurrence with channel-wise decay (Eq. 1 above), with a fixed-size state per head; the report describes KDA as replacing the growing key–value cache of softmax attention with this fixed recurrent state. K3 changes KDA's output gate from the low-rank form used in Kimi Linear to an input-dependent full-rank sigmoid gate.
  • Gated MLA layers. MLA as introduced in DeepSeek-V2, with an added output gate yt=Wo[σ(Wgxt)⊙õt]y_t=W_o[\sigma(W_gx_t)\odot\tilde o_t], and, following Kimi Linear's hybrid design, no positional encoding in these layers (NoPE). The report's stated reasoning is that the KDA layers supply position-sensitive and recency-aware mixing while the MLA layers supply unrestricted global content interaction.
  • Other documented parts of K3, not about attention: a Mixture-of-Experts feed-forward — many parallel sub-networks of which a router picks a few per token — with 896 routed experts of which 16 are active per token, and an additional mechanism the report calls Attention Residuals, which attends over depth, across layers, not over sequence positions.

In this note's vocabulary K3 therefore combines bucket (a), a recurrent fixed-state layer, with global attention layers whose cache is reduced by bucket (d), MLA. These are attributed to K3 because its own report states them, not because of the model's name or family. The two numbers that headline K3's parameter count differ slightly between documents (2.8T in the model card, 2.78T in the report's architecture table); this note does not rely on either. Any performance or speed claim for K3 comes from Moonshot's own materials and is not reproduced here.

Kimi K3.1. The K3.1 Pro announcement on Moonshot's site (kimi.ai/blog/kimi-k3-1-pro) describes it as a post-trained version of Kimi K3 and links no separate architecture document or model card. The attention details above are therefore documented for K3 only; for K3.1 this note makes no architecture claim beyond what that announcement states.

The source of KDA. KDA was introduced and studied in the Kimi Linear report (arXiv:2510.26692, a preprint, October 2025). The authors describe it as a refinement of Gated DeltaNet with finer-grained gating, used in a layerwise hybrid with MLA, and report, for their own 48B-total, 3B-active model, KV-cache use reduced by up to 75% and up to 6 times higher decoding throughput at 1M context relative to full MLA. Those are the authors' claims for that model and setup, not a general property of the method.

The wider pattern. Three documented designs now sit side by side. DeepSeek-V2 and V3 reduce the cache with MLA while keeping every layer global softmax attention. Kimi Linear and K3 keep a majority of layers as fixed-state recurrent layers and a minority as global attention. Grouped-query attention is the simplest cache reduction and is documented as a method, not tied to any one model here. Which design is better for a given task is an empirical question that depends on the model, data and context length; a fair comparison states nn, head and state dimensions, and training versus decoding, as in the cost section.

What to carry away

  • Attention is softmax(QK⊤/dk)V\mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})V; rows index readers, columns index what is read, and each row of the weight matrix sums to 1.
  • The n×nn\times n score matrix makes full attention O(n2)O(n^2) in training compute and, if stored, memory; decoding is O(n)O(n) per token plus reading the cache.
  • Ask first which of four things an efficient method changes: the mixing rule, the set of pairs, the execution, or the cache. FlashAttention only changes the third.
  • A fixed-size recurrent state buys linear cost and constant decode memory at the price of exact recall; hybrids keep some global layers for that reason.
  • Attribute a mechanism to a model only when its own report documents it, and date the claim.
